Hydrolyzed protein diets: how they work and what the label tells you

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Hydrolysis breaks proteins into small pieces the immune system is less likely to recognize. Less likely is not never: what studies found, what to ask.

Hydrolyzed protein diets are one of the main tools veterinarians use when a food allergy is suspected in a dog or cat. The ingredient list often names a familiar protein, such as "hydrolyzed chicken liver" or "hydrolyzed soy protein", which confuses owners: if the pet may react to chicken or soy, why is it in the food? This guide explains what hydrolysis does, what the research shows about its limits, and how to read these labels.

What hydrolysis does

A protein is a long chain of amino acids folded into a shape. In a food allergy, the immune system recognizes specific parts of that shape as a threat. Hydrolysis uses enzymes (and sometimes heat and acid) to cut the chain into smaller pieces called peptides. The idea is that if the pieces are small enough, the immune system no longer recognizes them as the original protein.

Two things follow from this:

  • The degree of hydrolysis matters. A partially hydrolyzed protein still contains larger fragments that may be recognized. An extensively hydrolyzed one is broken down further. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that extensively hydrolyzed diets are less likely to provoke clinically noticeable allergic responses, while partially hydrolyzed diets may still trigger reactions in some sensitized animals.
  • The source protein still matters. Hydrolysis lowers the chance of recognition; it doesn't turn the protein into something else. That's why many veterinary dermatologists prefer a hydrolyzed diet whose source protein the animal hasn't reacted to before.

What the research shows

The evidence supports hydrolyzed diets as useful, and it also shows they aren't a guarantee for every animal.

  • Systematic review (Olivry and Bizikova, 2010). Looking at dogs with cutaneous adverse food reactions, the authors found that in three controlled studies, up to half of the dogs showed worse clinical signs after eating partial hydrolysates made from foods they were already allergic to. Their conclusion was that hydrolysates are probably best used in dogs not suspected of being hypersensitive to the original protein.
  • Chicken-allergic dogs (Bizikova and Olivry, 2016). In a small, randomized, double-blinded crossover trial with ten dogs known to react to chicken, four of the ten had a flare of itching on a diet based on hydrolyzed chicken liver. None flared on a diet based on extensively hydrolyzed poultry feathers. The study was small and tested specific products, so it doesn't tell you how any other hydrolyzed diet will perform, but it shows that the source and the processing make a difference.
  • Undeclared ingredients (Ricci and colleagues, 2013). When researchers tested twelve dry limited-antigen diets (eleven novel-protein diets and one hydrolyzed diet) by microscopy and DNA analysis, only two matched their labels. The others contained animal material that wasn't declared, most often from birds. This is one reason vets often recommend specific veterinary diets for elimination trials and tell owners to be strict about everything else the pet eats.

Where you'll see hydrolyzed ingredients

Not every hydrolyzed ingredient on a label is there for allergy reasons. It helps to know the main places they appear:

WhereTypical label wordingWhy it's there
Veterinary hydrolyzed dietshydrolyzed soy protein, hydrolyzed chicken liver, hydrolyzed salmon protein, hydrolyzed poultry feathersMain protein source, chosen to reduce recognition
Everyday foodshydrolyzed chicken protein, hydrolyzed poultry proteinProtein source or flavor
Flavor coatingschicken digest, animal digestPalatability; a digest is hydrolyzed animal tissue
Mineral premixeszinc proteinate and other "proteinates"Minerals bound to amino acids, often made with hydrolyzed soy protein

A "hydrolyzed chicken protein" in an everyday adult food is not the same as a veterinary hydrolyzed diet. The label doesn't tell you how far the protein was broken down, and the rest of the recipe isn't designed around avoiding allergens.

Reading the rest of a hydrolyzed diet's label

The protein gets the attention, but the other ingredients matter during an elimination trial too:

  • Carbohydrate source. Many hydrolyzed diets use a purified starch, such as corn starch or rice starch. Purified starches carry very little protein, but if your vet has concerns about a specific plant, check the starch source.
  • Fats and oils. Fat sources such as vegetable oils or fish oil can appear. Highly refined fats carry very little protein, but very little is not none. Your vet can tell you whether it matters for your pet.
  • Flavor and coatings. Look for "natural flavor", "digest" or broth. In a diet built around a hydrolyzed protein, an unhydrolyzed flavor from another animal would undermine the point.
  • Product type. Hydrolyzed veterinary diets are complete foods meant to be fed on their own. Toppers, treats, chews and flavored medicines given alongside them change what your pet is actually eating.

How hydrolyzed diets are used

Veterinarians use hydrolyzed diets in two main ways:

  1. For an elimination trial. The pet eats only the hydrolyzed diet and water for a set period, usually several weeks, to see whether signs improve; then the old food or single ingredients are reintroduced to see whether they return. See choosing a food for an elimination diet for the label side of this.
  2. For long-term feeding after a food allergy has been identified, when a suitable diet with no problem ingredients is hard to find.

The choice between a hydrolyzed diet and a novel-protein diet, how long the trial lasts, and how to reintroduce foods are decisions for your vet. A hydrolyzed diet isn't the right tool for every pet, and a good result depends on feeding nothing else.

Questions to ask your vet

  • Has my pet reacted to the protein this diet is made from, or might it?
  • Is this a partially or extensively hydrolyzed diet?
  • Which treats, chews or flavored medicines can I use during the trial?
  • How long should we run the trial, and what should I record?
  • What do we do if signs flare after a reintroduction?
