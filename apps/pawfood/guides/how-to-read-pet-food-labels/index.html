How to read a dog or cat food label, step by step

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

A practical walk through every part of a pet food label: product name rules, ingredient order, guaranteed analysis, the adequacy statement, feeding guide and the small print.

The front of a bag is written to sell. The back is written to comply with feed law. If you want to know what is in the bowl, the back is where to look, and it follows a predictable layout in both the United States and the European Union. This guide walks through it in the order that saves the most time.

1. Start with what the food is for

Before the ingredients, find the nutritional adequacy statement (US) or the words complete feed / complementary feed (EU). This tells you whether the product is meant to be the whole diet.

  • A food described as "complete and balanced" (US) or "complete feed" (EU) is formulated to meet the nutrient profile for a species and life stage on its own.
  • A food "for intermittent or supplemental feeding only" (US) or a "complementary feed" (EU) is not. Treats, toppers and many mixers fall here.

The statement also names the life stage: growth, adult maintenance, gestation/lactation or all life stages. In the US it says whether the food was formulated to meet the AAFCO profiles or passed feeding trials. The guide to AAFCO and FEDIAF statements explains each wording.

If the product is not complete, everything else on the label matters less: it can't be the main diet anyway.

2. Read the product name literally

In the US, the name itself carries rules about how much of a named ingredient is in the food. The AAFCO "naming rules" work roughly like this (excluding water for processing):

Name on the packMinimum share of the named ingredient
"Chicken Dog Food"95% of the product (70% counting added water)
"Chicken Dinner", "Entrée", "Formula", "Platter"25%
"With Chicken"3%
"Chicken Flavor"Enough to be detected; no minimum percentage

EU labels follow a similar logic: if a name or picture highlights an ingredient, the label must state its percentage, and "with" signals a smaller share than a name built around the ingredient.

The practical lesson: "Lamb Formula" and "with Lamb" can be very different foods. And a name highlighting one protein says nothing about the others. A lamb recipe can still contain chicken fat or poultry meal.

3. Read the whole ingredient list

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight before processing. Three consequences:

  • Fresh meat is heavy with water. Chicken at the top of the list is about 70% water. After the food is dried, a chicken meal listed third may supply more of the protein.
  • Splitting moves things down. Several forms of one plant (for example peas, pea protein and pea fiber) each appear lower than they would as one combined ingredient.
  • Small amounts still count. Fats, flavors, digests and supplements sit at the end, but for a pet with an allergy they matter as much as the first ingredient. A list of thirty items needs reading to the last line.

When you check for something your pet avoids, look at every form: whole meat, meal, by-products, fat, broth, liver, digest, flavor and hydrolyzed protein. The chicken allergy checklist shows what that looks like for one species.

4. Watch for terms that don't name the species

Some legal terms are accurate but vague: animal fat, poultry fat, natural flavor, animal digest, meat meal and, on EU labels, meat and animal derivatives. They don't tell you which animal was used, and that can change between batches. If your pet has a protein on its avoid list, these are the lines to ask the manufacturer about. The guide on vague terms such as "poultry" has the full list.

5. Read the guaranteed analysis with care

The US guaranteed analysis gives crude protein (min.), crude fat (min.), crude fiber (max.) and moisture (max.). EU labels list analytical constituents: protein, fat content, crude fibre and crude ash, plus moisture when it exceeds 14%.

Three things to keep in mind:

  1. Minimums and maximums are not exact values. A food with "crude protein min. 26%" might contain 28%.
  2. "Crude" refers to the lab method, not quality. Crude protein is calculated from nitrogen and does not distinguish animal from plant protein.
  3. Water hides the real proportions. A wet food with 10% protein and a dry food with 26% protein cannot be compared directly. Convert both to dry matter, or use the dry matter calculator.

Carbohydrates are almost never listed. They can only be estimated when protein, fat, fiber, moisture and ash are all printed. When ash is missing, there is no honest estimate; a missing value is not zero.

6. Check the calorie statement and feeding guide

US labels state calories as kcal per kg and usually per cup or can. EU labels often give energy per 100 g. Calorie density varies a lot between foods, so a switch can change how much your pet eats even at the same volume.

The feeding guide is a starting point based on weight. Real needs vary with age, activity, neuter status and health. Watch your pet's weight and body condition, and ask your vet before changing amounts for a pet with a health condition.

7. The small print

  • Manufacturer or distributor name and address. This is who to contact with ingredient questions, such as which bird is in "poultry fat".
  • Lot number and best-before date. Useful if you ever need to compare against a recall notice. Recall notices are usually brand- and lot-specific; a notice for a brand doesn't mean every bag is affected.
  • Additives. EU labels list technological and nutritional additives in separate sections, sometimes with E-numbers. Preservatives such as mixed tocopherols or rosemary extract may appear in brackets after the fat they protect.

A five-minute label routine

  1. Complete or complementary? Which life stage?
  2. Read the product name literally.
  3. Read the whole ingredient list, including the last line.
  4. Circle vague terms if your pet avoids a protein.
  5. Convert the guaranteed analysis to dry matter if you are comparing foods.

Frequently asked questions

Is the first ingredient the main ingredient?

It is the heaviest ingredient before cooking. If it is fresh meat, much of that weight is water, so a meal or plant protein further down can contribute more of the finished food's protein.

Why does the label say "crude" protein?

"Crude" names the lab method: protein is calculated from the nitrogen content. It is not a quality grade, and it includes both animal and plant protein.

Where are the carbohydrates?

Labels rarely list them. You can estimate carbohydrates by difference only when protein, fat, fiber, moisture and ash are all given.
