Most commonly reported food allergens in cats, and where they hide on labels

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Beef, fish and chicken top the published cat cases. What the numbers mean, why they aren't a risk ranking, and where those proteins hide on cat food labels.

If your vet suspects a food allergy in your cat, one of the first questions is which foods to look at. Published case reports give a useful starting point, as long as you read them for what they are. This guide summarizes what the research found, explains the limits of those numbers, and shows where the most often reported proteins can appear on a cat food label under less obvious names.

What the research found

The most cited summary is a 2016 critically appraised topic by Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud in BMC Veterinary Research. The authors pooled published reports of cats whose food allergy was confirmed by an elimination diet followed by a reintroduction. Across 78 cats, the food sources reported were:

Food sourceCatsShare of the 78 cats
Beef1418%
Fish1317%
Chicken45%
Wheat34%
Corn34%
Dairy products34%
Lamb23%
Egg, barley, rabbit1 eachn/a

Some cats reacted to more than one food, so the shares don't add up to 100%.

How to read these numbers

These figures are often quoted as "the top cat allergens". That's a fair summary, with three caveats the authors themselves point out:

  • They aren't prevalence figures. The table counts positive reintroductions in published cases. Most cats were never tested against every food, so the true numbers for each source are likely higher, and the table can't tell you how likely any one cat is to react to beef or fish.
  • Exposure shapes the list. Beef, fish and chicken have long been common in cat foods. An animal can only develop an allergy to something it has eaten, so the most fed proteins tend to top these lists.
  • The sample is small. 78 cats across many studies is a modest number. The gap between beef and fish (one cat) is not meaningful.

The practical takeaway is narrower than "avoid beef and fish": these are the sources a vet is most likely to consider first, and the ones worth knowing how to spot on a label.

How food allergy tends to look in cats

A 2019 review of 210 cats with food reactions (Olivry and Mueller) found that most were itchy, mainly on the head, face and neck, with the belly and ears also often involved. Common patterns were hair loss from overgrooming, scabby lesions on the head and neck, small crusty bumps (miliary dermatitis) and eosinophilic skin lesions. Cats appeared to develop signs later in life than dogs, and no breed or sex stood out. Digestive signs such as vomiting or diarrhea can occur too.

None of these signs is specific to food. Fleas, environmental allergies and other conditions look very similar, which is why your vet will want to rule those out and run a proper diet trial. See food allergy or food intolerance?.

Where beef, fish and chicken hide on cat food labels

Cat foods, especially wet foods, often combine several animal sources. A product named after one protein can contain others.

Fish

  • Named fish: tuna, salmon, whitefish, sardine, mackerel
  • Non-specific terms: fish, ocean fish, seafood, fish by-products
  • Less obvious forms: fish oil, fish digest, fish broth, fish gelatin

Fish oil is a refined fat and carries far less protein than fish meat, but it isn't protein-free. If fish is on your cat's avoid list, ask your vet whether fish oil matters too.

Beef

  • Named: beef, beef liver, beef broth, beef by-products
  • Non-specific: "meat", meat and animal derivatives (common on EU labels), "meat by-products", animal digest, animal fat
  • Gelling agents: gelatin can come from beef or pork and the label often doesn't say which

Chicken

  • Named: chicken, chicken meal, chicken liver, chicken fat, hydrolyzed chicken protein
  • Non-specific: "poultry", poultry meal, poultry by-products, poultry digest. "Poultry" may include chicken but doesn't have to; see ambiguous terms like poultry.

Dairy

  • Milk, cheese, whey, casein, and "cat milk" products. These turn up more in treats and complementary products than in main foods.

Product names can mislead

US product naming rules, summarized by AAFCO, work by percentage:

  • "Tuna Cat Food": tuna makes up at least 95% of the product by weight, not counting added water (and at least 70% counting it).
  • "Tuna Dinner", "Entrée" or "Formula": tuna is at least 25% of the product excluding water.
  • "With Tuna": only at least 3% tuna.
  • "Tuna Flavor": tuna only needs to be detectable.

So a "with salmon" recipe may be built mostly on chicken or another protein. The ingredient list, not the name, is what tells you which animals are in the can. EU labels follow different naming rules, but the same lesson applies.

If you're working with your vet on a food allergy

  • Bring the packaging, or a photo of the full ingredient list, of everything your cat eats, including treats and medicine pockets.
  • Ask your vet which proteins to avoid, and at what level of strictness (for example, whether fats and oils from the same animal matter).
  • During an elimination trial, feed nothing but the diet your vet chose. Cats that hunt or go outdoors make trials harder; ask your vet how to handle that.
  • After a reintroduction, signs often return quickly in cats: in one review, more than 90% of cats flared within 7 days (Olivry and Mueller, 2020).
