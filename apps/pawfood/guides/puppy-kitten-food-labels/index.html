Puppy and kitten food labels: growth, all life stages and large breeds

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

What to check on a puppy or kitten food label, from the growth statement and the large-breed wording to calories, calcium and feeding guides.

Puppies and kittens grow fast, and they need more of some nutrients than adults do. The good news is that the most important information on a growth food is printed in one short sentence on the back of the bag. The less obvious news is that the word "puppy" or "kitten" on the front is not that sentence. This guide shows you what to look for and what to ask your vet about.

The sentence that matters: the nutritional adequacy statement

A complete food says which life stage it is designed for. In the United States this is the AAFCO nutritional adequacy statement; in Europe, labels state that a food is a complete feed and for which animal and life stage, following FEDIAF's guidelines. (We explain both in AAFCO and FEDIAF statements.)

For a young animal, look for one of these:

Statement saysSuitable for a growing puppy or kitten?
"growth"Yes, designed for growth
"all life stages"Yes; it covers growth and reproduction as well as adults
"adult maintenance"No, designed for adults only
"for intermittent or supplemental feeding only" / complementary feedNot a complete diet on its own

Brand names and front-of-pack wording ("puppy formula", "junior", "kitten recipe") are marketing. A bag that says "puppy" on the front should also say "growth" or "all life stages" in its statement. If you can't find a statement, the food may not be complete, and it is worth asking the manufacturer or your vet before using it as the main diet.

Large-breed puppies: the extra line to check

Puppies of large and giant breeds grow for longer and are more sensitive to how much energy and calcium they get while they grow. AAFCO's labelling rules address this directly. For dog foods that claim growth or all life stages, the statement says whether the food is suitable for the growth of large-size dogs, defined as dogs expected to weigh 70 lb (32 kg) or more as adults. You will see wording like:

  • "...including growth of large size dogs (70 lb. or more as an adult)", or
  • "...except for growth of large size dogs (70 lb. or more as an adult)".

If your puppy is likely to be large, the first wording is the one to look for. If you are not sure how big your puppy will become, for example with a mixed breed, ask your vet.

FEDIAF's nutritional guidelines also give separate recommendations for early and late growth in puppies and pay particular attention to calcium in large breeds. Labels in Europe may describe this as a food for "large breed puppies"; read the statement and the feeding guide together.

Don't add calcium on your own

A complete growth food is already balanced for calcium and phosphorus. Adding calcium supplements, bone or large amounts of extras can tip that balance. If you think your puppy needs a supplement, ask your vet first.

Kittens: the cat-specific points

Kittens use the same "growth" or "all life stages" statements. A few cat-specific things are worth knowing:

  • Taurine. Cats need taurine in their diet, and complete cat foods include it. See cats and taurine.
  • Dog food is not cat food. Cats have different nutrient needs, so a dog growth food is not a substitute.
  • Wet and dry both count. Many kittens eat a mix. Check that each complete food has a suitable statement and compare them on a dry matter basis, not on the raw label numbers.

Calories and the feeding guide

Growing animals need energy, but too much can be a problem, particularly for large-breed puppies. Two parts of the label help:

  • Calorie content. US labels state calories as kcal per kg and often per cup or can. This lets you compare foods properly; two foods can need very different amounts.
  • Feeding guide. The table on the bag is a starting point by age and expected or current weight. It can't know your pet's breed, activity or body condition.

Your vet can check your puppy's or kitten's body condition at visits and tell you whether to feed more or less than the guide suggests. Weighing regularly and writing it down makes those conversations easier.

Reading the guaranteed analysis for growth foods

Growth foods often show higher protein and fat than adult foods. Some also list calcium, phosphorus or DHA (an omega-3 fatty acid, often from fish oil). A few tips:

  • Compare like with like. Wet foods look low in everything because they are mostly water. Convert to dry matter before comparing.
  • Minimums and maximums. Guaranteed analysis values are usually minimums (protein, fat) or maximums (fibre, moisture), not exact amounts.
  • Not reported is not zero. If a value isn't on the label, it simply wasn't declared.

Treats, chews and growing up

Treats for young animals add calories and can upset the balance of a growth diet. Keep them small and few; the WSAVA nutrition guidelines suggest treats should stay a small share of daily calories. Read their ingredient lists too, especially if your vet has raised any concern about food reactions (see treats and toppers).

When to move to adult food

There is no single right age. Smaller breeds generally finish growing earlier than large and giant breeds, and kittens have their own timeline. Your vet is the right person to tell you when to switch. When you do, change gradually (see switching foods) and check that the new food's statement says "adult maintenance" or "all life stages".

A quick checklist

  1. Statement says growth or all life stages.
  2. For a puppy that may be 70 lb (32 kg) or more as an adult: includes growth of large size dogs.
  3. Calorie content is shown, and you know roughly how much to feed.
  4. No extra calcium unless your vet recommends it.
  5. Treats are small, few and checked.
  6. Weight and body condition are tracked and discussed with your vet.
