Preservatives in pet food: mixed tocopherols, BHA, BHT and ethoxyquin

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

A neutral look at why pet foods contain preservatives, how natural and synthetic antioxidants differ, what the rules say and how to read them on a label.

"Preserved with mixed tocopherols." "Contains BHA." "No artificial preservatives." Preservatives are one of the most emotionally loaded topics on a pet food label, and also one of the least explained. This guide doesn't tell you which option to choose. It explains what these ingredients do, how they differ, what regulators have decided, and what a label can and cannot tell you.

Why pet food needs preservatives at all

Most dry pet foods contain added fat, often animal fat or fish oil, for energy and essential fatty acids. Fats react with oxygen over time. This process, called oxidation, makes fats go rancid: the smell and taste change, and fat-soluble nutrients such as some vitamins break down.

Preservatives that slow oxidation are called antioxidants. They are not there to kill bacteria; they protect fats. Canned and pouched wet foods are handled differently: they are heat-treated in a sealed container, so they usually rely much less on added antioxidants (see wet vs dry food labels).

Some foods also contain preservatives that slow mould or yeast growth, such as potassium sorbate or calcium propionate. They are more common in semi-moist foods and treats than in plain kibble.

"Natural" here describes where the substance comes from, not how well it works or whether it suits your pet. Both groups are used for the same job.

Mixed tocopherols and their companions

Tocopherols are a family of compounds that together make up vitamin E. As a preservative, a blend of them ("mixed tocopherols") is added to fats to slow rancidity. You will often see them together with rosemary extract and citric acid, which help in the same task.

A few useful details:

  • Preservative or nutrient? A label can list both "mixed tocopherols (a preservative)" and "vitamin E supplement". The first protects the fat; the second is part of the vitamin premix that makes the food nutritionally complete. They are related compounds with different roles.
  • Shorter protection. Natural-source antioxidants are generally considered less long-lasting than synthetic ones, which is one reason storage and the best-before date matter more for foods that use them.
  • Source. Tocopherols and vitamin E supplements are often made from vegetable oils, sometimes soybean oil. The amount of protein that carries over is usually considered minimal, but if your vet has asked you to avoid soy very strictly, it is reasonable to ask the manufacturer about the source.

BHA, BHT, TBHQ and propyl gallate

These synthetic antioxidants have been used in human food, animal feed and cosmetics for decades. They work well at low levels and are stable through cooking and storage.

They are also the subject of ongoing debate. Regulators set conditions of use and maximum levels for them, and those conditions are not identical everywhere. Some owners avoid them on principle; others accept them because they are authorised and used at low levels. Both positions exist, and a label won't settle the question for you. If you have a specific concern for your pet, for example a medical condition, that is a conversation for your vet.

Ethoxyquin: the one with a clear regulatory change

Ethoxyquin is a synthetic antioxidant that has been used widely in animal feed, including to stabilise fish meal during storage and transport.

In the European Union, its status changed. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/1375 denied the authorisation of ethoxyquin as a feed additive. The decision followed an evaluation by EFSA, the EU's food authority, which could not reach a conclusion on its use because data were missing, including on an impurity called p-phenetidine. Transitional periods allowed existing stocks to be used up.

In other regions ethoxyquin can still be permitted under specific conditions. That is why you may still see it on some labels or in ingredient discussions online.

What this means in practice:

  • A regulatory denial reflects missing evidence and unresolved questions in an assessment. It is not the same thing as proof of harm at the levels used.
  • If you buy food from more than one region, or imported products, you may see different preservative choices for the same type of food.

What the label shows, and what it may not

Rules for declaring preservatives differ between regions:

  • United States. AAFCO's labelling guidance expects added preservatives to be declared in the ingredient list, often with a note such as "(a preservative)" or "preserved with".
  • European Union. Under Regulation (EC) No 767/2009, pet food labels may group some technological additives. You may see a general phrase such as "antioxidants" or a category name instead of every substance listed individually.
  • Preservatives in raw materials. An ingredient such as fish meal or animal fat may have been stabilised by its supplier before it reached the pet food factory. How, and whether, such additives appear on the final label depends on local rules. If this matters to you, the manufacturer is the only one who can answer.

So "no artificial preservatives" on the front of a bag is a marketing claim about the finished recipe. Read the ingredient list and, if you have a specific question, contact the manufacturer and write down what they told you and when.

Storage matters whichever preservative is used

Preservatives slow oxidation; they don't stop it. A few habits help any dry food stay in better condition:

  • Check the best-before date when you buy, especially for larger bags.
  • Keep the food in its original bag (it is designed as a barrier, and it carries the lot number and date), and close it tightly.
  • Store it somewhere cool and dry, away from direct sun.
  • If the food smells clearly different from usual, stop and contact the manufacturer or your retailer.

Reading preservatives calmly

  1. Find the preservative in the ingredient list, often near the fat ingredient ("chicken fat, preserved with mixed tocopherols").
  2. Look for the category on EU labels if no substance is named.
  3. Separate preference from evidence. Choosing natural-source antioxidants is a legitimate preference; it is not a medical requirement unless your vet says so.
  4. Ask your vet if your pet has a condition that makes you want to avoid a specific additive.
