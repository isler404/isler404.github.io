\"Poultry\", \"animal fat\", \"natural flavor\": vague pet food label terms explained

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Which pet food label terms don't name the animal, what each one can contain, why it matters for allergy lists, and how to get a straight answer from the manufacturer.

Most pet food ingredient names tell you two things: which animal or plant the ingredient comes from and how it was processed. "Chicken meal" is chicken, rendered. "Salmon oil" is salmon, pressed and refined. A small group of legal names only tells you the second half. They are accurate, permitted and common, but for a pet with a protein on its avoid list they leave the key question open.

Why these terms exist

Feed law allows collective names because manufacturers buy raw materials on the open market and the exact species can vary with price and supply. A term like "poultry fat" lets one recipe use chicken fat in one batch and turkey fat in another without reprinting the bag. For most pets that makes no difference. For a pet whose veterinarian has confirmed a reaction to chicken, it is the difference between a match and an unknown.

The terms to look for

Poultry terms

Poultry, poultry meal, poultry fat, poultry by-product meal, poultry digest, natural poultry flavor and hydrolyzed poultry protein.

"Poultry" can be chicken, turkey, duck, goose or other domesticated birds. Chicken is by far the most widely used, but the label doesn't promise it.

Mammal terms

Meat, meat meal (meat and bone meal), meat by-products, animal fat, tallow, liver, gelatin, animal plasma and broth.

These usually come from cattle, pigs, sheep or goats. "Animal fat" and "animal digest" can also include poultry.

Fish terms

Fish, fish meal, ocean fish, whitefish and fish oil without a species. A pet that reacts to one fish species may or may not react to others; your vet will want to know which species were present.

Flavors and digests

Natural flavor and animal digest are often made by breaking down animal tissue. They are used in small amounts but carry protein from an unnamed source. A named flavor ("natural chicken flavor") tells you the main source.

EU category names

EU law allows labels to list ingredients by category instead of by name. The ones that matter most for allergy lists are meat and animal derivatives, fish and fish derivatives, derivatives of vegetable origin, cereals, and oils and fats. A label may add "(of which chicken 4%)": that tells you chicken is present, not that it is the only animal.

Plant terms

Vegetable oil, vegetable protein extracts and hydrolyzed vegetable protein don't name the plant. They matter if your pet avoids soy, wheat or corn.

How much does it matter?

It depends on two things your vet can help with.

How strictly your pet must avoid the protein. During an elimination trial, every source counts, including small amounts. For an owner who prefers to feed less chicken, a vague fat line may be acceptable.

How much protein the form carries. Rendered fats and refined oils carry much less protein than meat or meal, while digests, flavors, meals and broths carry it fully. Pawfood records this as protein carryover on every ingredient page. Less protein is not no protein, and no label states the amount.

How to get an answer

Manufacturers are usually willing to say which species a vague ingredient contains. A short, specific question works best:

  1. Name the product exactly, with the size and the lot number or best-before date from the bag.
  2. Ask about each vague line: "Which animal species are in the 'animal fat' and 'natural flavor' in this product?"
  3. Ask whether the answer is fixed or can change between batches.
  4. Ask whether the product is made on lines shared with recipes containing the protein you avoid. Shared equipment isn't listed on labels.

Keep the reply with your records. If the answer is "it varies", that is useful too: for a strict avoid list, it means the ingredient can't be ruled out.

Frequently asked questions

Is "poultry" always chicken?

No. It is often chicken, but it can be turkey, duck or other domesticated birds, and the mix can change between batches. Only the manufacturer can tell you.

Are vague ingredients lower quality?

Not necessarily. The term is about naming, not quality. The issue for allergy lists is that the species is unknown.

My pet is fine with the food. Should I worry about vague terms?

If your pet does well and has no avoid list, vague terms may never matter. They matter when you need to know exactly which proteins your pet eats, for example during an elimination trial your vet has set up.
