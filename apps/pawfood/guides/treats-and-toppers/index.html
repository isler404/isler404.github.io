How treats and toppers can undo an allergy diet

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Treats, chews, toppers and flavoured extras often carry the very proteins a diet avoids. Here is how to check them and keep a diet trial consistent.

You read the main food's label carefully. You picked something without the ingredient your vet asked you to avoid. Then a treat, a spoon of gravy or a flavoured chew brings that ingredient straight back into the bowl. This is one of the most common reasons an allergy diet or an elimination trial is hard to interpret, and it is almost always fixable once you know where to look.

Why the extras matter so much

Food allergies in dogs and cats are reactions to specific components of food, usually proteins. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes the elimination diet trial as the way vets investigate a suspected food allergy, and stresses that it has to be strict: during the trial the pet should eat only the chosen diet. A small amount of the wrong protein, given regularly, can be enough to make the result hard to read.

From the owner's side, the main food is usually the easy part. The hard part is everything else:

  • training treats and biscuits
  • dental chews and long-lasting chews
  • wet food "toppers", broths and gravies
  • flavoured supplements and chewable medicines
  • bits of human food, from the table or from children
  • the other pet's food bowl

Treats are a different kind of product

Most treats are not designed to be a full diet. In the United States, AAFCO guidance says a product that is not complete and balanced must carry a statement such as "for intermittent or supplemental feeding only". In the EU the equivalent is a complementary feed, which is meant to be fed alongside a complete food (see AAFCO and FEDIAF statements).

That has two consequences:

  1. They shift the balance of the diet. The WSAVA Global Nutrition Guidelines suggest keeping treats to a small share of daily calories, commonly given as no more than about 10%, so the complete food still provides most of the nutrition. Toppers count too.
  2. Their ingredient lists deserve the same attention as the main food. A treat is often where a "hidden" protein turns up.

A review of adverse food reactions by Mueller and colleagues (2016) reports that the sources most often involved in dogs include beef, dairy, chicken and wheat, and in cats beef, fish and chicken. These are exactly the ingredients that turn up in small amounts in treats and toppers.

Watch especially for non-specific terms: "poultry", "meat", "animal digest", "animal fat", "natural flavour" and, on European labels, "meat and animal derivatives". They don't name the animal (see ambiguous poultry terms). Gelatin and collagen also come from animals, and their source is often not named.

Toppers: small amounts, big effect

Toppers are designed to make a meal more interesting. That is the problem during an allergy diet: they add new ingredients to a carefully chosen food.

  • A topper labelled with one meat can contain broth or fat from another.
  • Toppers change the overall nutrient balance, which matters if your pet is on a therapeutic diet.
  • If you need to tempt a picky eater, ask your vet first. Some diets come with a matching wet version or an approved way to add flavour.

The "not food" items people forget

  • Flavoured medicines and supplements. Chewable tablets and some supplements are flavoured with meat or other proteins. Don't stop or change a medicine on your own; tell your vet about the diet and ask whether a different form is possible.
  • Toothpaste for pets may be flavoured, for example with poultry.
  • Pill pockets and treat pastes used to give tablets.
  • Licking plates and other pets' bowls, especially in multi-pet homes.

Human food extras

Owners often switch to "plain" human food as a treat, which brings its own considerations. Cheese adds dairy; cooked chicken is chicken. Some foods are a problem for pets regardless of allergies: the ASPCA lists foods to avoid such as grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, chocolate and macadamia nuts. Peanut butter is a common pill-hider; the U.S. FDA warns dog owners about xylitol, a sweetener found in some peanut butters and other products, so check the label before using it.

A practical plan for treat-proofing a diet

  1. Agree on a treat list with your vet. Often the simplest option is pieces of the diet food itself, or a treat made for the same diet.
  2. Read every extra like a main food. Check all animal ingredients, not just the headline one.
  3. Put a note on the treat jar. Everyone who feeds the pet needs to know the rules, including visitors and children.
  4. Use a separate feeding routine if you have more than one pet.
  5. Log every extra, however small, with date and amount. If symptoms change, you and your vet can see what else was eaten.
  6. Before ending or changing a diet trial, talk to your vet. Reintroducing foods is usually part of the plan, and your vet decides how.
