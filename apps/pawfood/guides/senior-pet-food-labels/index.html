Senior pet food labels: what "senior" does and doesn't tell you

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

There is no separate legal standard for senior pet food. Learn what to check on the label instead, from calories and protein to joint claims and missing values.

As dogs and cats get older, many owners reach for a bag that says "senior", "mature" or "7+". It feels like the obvious step. But the word on the front is less informative than it seems, and two "senior" foods can be very different. This guide explains what the label really guarantees for an older pet, and which parts are worth reading closely before you talk to your vet about a change.

There is no official "senior" standard

AAFCO's nutrient profiles, which US labels refer to, cover two groups: growth and reproduction, and adult maintenance. There is no separate senior profile. So a senior food in the US is legally an adult maintenance (or all life stages) food. You can check this in its nutritional adequacy statement (see AAFCO and FEDIAF statements).

In Europe the situation is similar in practice: foods for older pets are complete feeds for adult dogs or cats, formulated with FEDIAF's nutritional guidelines in mind. "Senior" describes who the product is aimed at, not a separate set of rules.

What this means:

  • Manufacturers choose their own approach for "senior". Some lower the calories, some raise the protein, some add joint ingredients or extra fibre, and some do several of these.
  • The age on the bag ("7+", "11+") is the manufacturer's choice, not a standard. Pets age at different rates; large dogs are often considered older at a younger age than small ones.
  • A food doesn't need to say "senior" to suit an older pet, and a "senior" food doesn't automatically suit every older pet.

Start with your pet, not the bag

The WSAVA Global Nutrition Guidelines recommend a nutritional assessment as part of every vet visit: body weight, body condition score, muscle condition, diet history and any medical conditions. That assessment is a better guide to food choice than the age on the bag.

Things to bring up with your vet:

  • Has your pet gained or lost weight? Some older pets gain weight, others lose it; both are worth a conversation.
  • Is muscle being lost along the back or hips?
  • Has appetite, thirst, chewing or stool changed?
  • Does your pet have a diagnosed condition, or take medicine?

If your pet has a medical condition, your vet may recommend a therapeutic diet. A "senior" food from the shop is not a replacement for that, and choosing a lower-protein food on your own "to protect the kidneys", for example, is a decision to make with your vet, not from a label.

What to read on a senior label

1. The nutritional adequacy statement

Look for "adult maintenance" or "all life stages", and check that it names the right species. If a product says "for intermittent or supplemental feeding only" or "complementary feed", it is not meant to be the whole diet.

2. Calorie content

Calories vary a great deal between senior foods. US labels show kcal per kg and often per cup or can. If your pet is gaining weight, a food with fewer calories per cup can help keep portions familiar; if your pet is losing weight, the opposite may be true. Your vet can tell you which direction to go.

3. Protein and fat, on a dry matter basis

Guaranteed analysis values are minimums or maximums on an "as fed" basis. To compare a wet senior food with a dry one, or two different senior foods, convert to dry matter. Protein ranges widely across senior products; one isn't automatically right for every older pet.

4. Fibre and moisture

Some senior foods add fibre, for example powdered cellulose or beet pulp, which can change stool and how full a pet feels. Moisture matters too: wet food adds water to the diet (see wet vs dry food labels).

5. Values that are missing

Phosphorus, sodium, calcium and other minerals are often not in the guaranteed analysis. If a value isn't on the label, it is not reported, not zero. If your vet asks about a specific nutrient, the manufacturer can usually give you a typical analysis.

Joint and "healthy ageing" ingredients

Senior foods often highlight glucosamine, chondroitin, omega-3 fatty acids (from fish oil), L-carnitine or antioxidants. A few things to keep in mind:

  • The label often lists these ingredients without saying how much is present. Some manufacturers add the amount to the guaranteed analysis; many don't.
  • A front-of-pack claim such as "supports joints" is a marketing claim, not a treatment.
  • If your pet already takes a joint supplement or fish oil from your vet, tell them about the food too, so the total is clear.

Practical points for older eaters

  • Texture. Pets with dental problems may manage softer food or smaller kibble more easily. Ask your vet if eating seems uncomfortable.
  • Smell and appetite. Warming wet food slightly or feeding smaller, more frequent meals helps some pets; ask your vet before adding toppers, especially on a therapeutic diet.
  • Change gradually. Older digestive systems can be less tolerant of sudden changes (see switching foods).
  • Write things down. Small changes in weight, appetite or stool are easy to forget between visits.

A short checklist before switching to a "senior" food

  1. Ask your vet whether your pet needs a change at all, and in which direction (calories, protein, texture).
  2. Check the statement: adult maintenance or all life stages, right species.
  3. Compare calories and dry matter values with the current food.
  4. Note any missing values you may need to ask the manufacturer about.
  5. Check the full ingredient list against anything your pet should avoid.
  6. Switch gradually and track weight, appetite and stool.
