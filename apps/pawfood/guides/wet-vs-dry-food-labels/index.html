Wet vs dry pet food: how to compare the labels fairly

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Wet food looks low in protein because it is mostly water. Learn to compare canned and dry labels on a dry matter and calorie basis, step by step.

A can of wet food that says "crude protein min 9%" and a bag of kibble that says "crude protein min 26%" look like two very different products. Often they are much closer than they seem, and sometimes the wet food is the higher-protein option. The difference is water. This guide shows how to put wet and dry labels on the same footing so the comparison means something.

Why the printed numbers can't be compared directly

The guaranteed analysis on a US label (called "analytical constituents" on EU labels) is expressed as fed: the percentage of each nutrient in the food exactly as it comes out of the package, water included. AAFCO lists four basic guarantees: minimum crude protein, minimum crude fat, maximum crude fiber and maximum moisture.

Wet foods are mostly water. Dry foods contain very little. When a large share of the food is water, every other nutrient is "diluted" on paper. Comparing as-fed percentages between a can and a bag is like comparing the sugar in fruit juice and dried fruit by the glass: the units are the same, but the products are not.

There are two fair ways to compare:

  1. Dry matter basis: remove the water mathematically and compare what is left. Our dry matter guide covers the idea in general, and the dry matter calculator does the arithmetic.
  2. Energy basis: compare how much of a nutrient comes with a fixed number of calories.

Both are useful. Dry matter is easier to calculate from the label alone; the energy basis is closer to what your pet actually eats, because pets eat to meet their calorie needs, not to finish a certain weight of food.

Step 1: Find the moisture value

Look for "moisture (max)" in the guaranteed analysis or analytical constituents. If moisture isn't printed, you can't calculate dry matter accurately. Don't guess a number and don't treat a missing value as zero. Check the manufacturer's website for a "typical analysis", or ask them.

Step 2: Convert to dry matter

The formula is the same for every nutrient:

nutrient on a dry matter basis (%) = nutrient as fed (%) ÷ (100 − moisture %) × 100

A worked example with two illustrative labels (not real products):

Label value (as fed)Wet foodDry food
Crude protein (min)9%26%
Crude fat (min)5%15%
Crude fiber (max)1%4%
Moisture (max)78%10%
Dry matter100 − 78 = 22%100 − 10 = 90%
Protein, dry matter basis9 ÷ 22 × 100 ≈ 40.9%26 ÷ 90 × 100 ≈ 28.9%
Fat, dry matter basis5 ÷ 22 × 100 ≈ 22.7%15 ÷ 90 × 100 ≈ 16.7%
Fiber, dry matter basis1 ÷ 22 × 100 ≈ 4.5%4 ÷ 90 × 100 ≈ 4.4%

On the shelf, the wet food looked like it had about a third of the protein. Once the water is removed, it has more protein and more fat per unit of solids than the kibble.

Step 3: Remember what "min" and "max" mean

Guaranteed analysis values are limits, not measurements. "Protein min 26%" promises at least 26%; the real figure may be higher. "Fiber max 4%" promises no more than 4%; the real figure may be lower. When you convert two labels, you are comparing their guarantees, which is useful but not exact.

Some EU labels print "typical" values instead of minimums and maximums. Typical values are closer to the average of what's in the food, but they are not guarantees either. Avoid mixing a typical value from one product with a guaranteed minimum from another without noting it.

Step 4: Compare on calories when you can

In the US, labels of complete and balanced dog and cat foods carry a calorie statement, and many European labels show energy too, usually in kcal per kg and per can, cup or serving. It lets you go one step further:

grams of nutrient per 1,000 kcal = (nutrient as fed % × 10,000) ÷ kcal per kg as fed

For the illustrative dry food above, with a calorie statement of 3,600 kcal/kg: 26 × 10,000 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 72 g of protein per 1,000 kcal.

This shows how much protein, fat or fiber comes along with the calories your pet needs. It matters most when two foods differ a lot in fat, because fat is energy-dense and changes how much food your pet eats.

You don't have to do this for every comparison. Dry matter is often enough to spot whether two foods are broadly similar or clearly different. Use calories when you are comparing for weight management or when fat levels differ a lot.

Things that don't show up in the guaranteed analysis

Converting the numbers fixes the water problem, but a few differences between wet and dry foods need a second look:

  • Ingredient order. Ingredients are listed from most to least by weight. In wet foods, water, broth or a named meat with high moisture often comes first. A water-rich fresh meat can rank high on the list even if it contributes less dry protein than a "meal" further down. Read the list as a whole, not just the first item.
  • Thickeners and gelling agents. Wet foods often use ingredients such as guar gum, cassia gum or carrageenan to set the texture. They are present in small amounts; if your vet has asked you to avoid any of them, check every can, because recipes in the same range can differ.
  • Gravy, broth and flavor. Broth and gravy can be made from an animal source that isn't the one in the product name. "Chicken" in the name doesn't rule out beef broth in the list, so read the full list when you're avoiding a specific protein.
  • Adequacy statement. A wet food may be a complete food or a complementary one (a topper or snack). Check the nutritional adequacy statement before treating two products as alternatives. A complementary product isn't meant to be the only food.
  • Feeding amount. Because wet food is so water-rich, the daily amount by weight is much larger. Compare feeding guides on calories, not grams.

Mixing wet and dry

Many owners feed both. That's fine as long as the total calories stay where your pet needs them and the main food is complete for your pet's life stage. When you add wet food, reduce the dry portion by a matching number of calories, not by the same weight. If your pet has a health condition, weight goal or a vet-supervised diet, ask your vet how to split the portions. If you are moving from one food to another rather than mixing, see switching foods.

A short checklist

  • Find moisture on both labels. If one is missing, note it instead of guessing.
  • Convert protein, fat and fiber to dry matter with the same formula.
  • Keep "min", "max" and "typical" values apart.
  • Use kcal per kg to compare per 1,000 kcal when fat differs a lot.
  • Read the full ingredient list of each product, including broth and gravy.
  • Confirm both products are complete foods for the same species and life stage.
