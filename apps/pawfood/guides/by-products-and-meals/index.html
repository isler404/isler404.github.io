What "by-product" and "meal" really mean on a pet food label

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

By-products and meals are defined feed terms, not insults. Learn what they include, why the species name matters and how to read them for allergies.

Few words on a pet food bag start as many arguments as "by-product" and "meal". Some owners read them as warning signs. Both reactions skip the useful part: these are defined feed terms, and each one tells you something specific about where an ingredient came from and how it was processed. Once you know what they mean, you can read them for what matters to your pet, including which animal they come from.

Why these words exist at all

In the United States, ingredient names on pet food are largely based on definitions maintained by the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO). A name like "chicken meal" is not a casual description; it refers to a definition that says what the ingredient may and may not contain. In the European Union, Regulation (EC) No 767/2009 sets the general labelling rules and allows ingredients to be declared either by name or by category, which is why you will see broader terms such as "meat and animal derivatives" on European labels.

So when you see these words, the first question is not "is this good or bad?" but "which definition is this, and what does it leave open?"

What a "meal" is

A meal is an ingredient that has been rendered: the raw material is cooked, the fat is separated, and the water is driven off. What is left is ground into a dry, concentrated powder. That is why meals are common in kibble: they are easy to store and handle, and they carry a lot of protein per gram compared with fresh meat, which is mostly water.

This has a practical effect on how you read an ingredient list:

  • Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight before processing, according to AAFCO's consumer guidance on labels.
  • Fresh "chicken" is weighed with its water, so it can appear first on a list even if, after cooking and drying, it contributes less to the finished food than a meal listed further down.
  • "Chicken meal" has already lost most of its water, so its position reflects a more concentrated ingredient.

Neither order is a trick in itself. It just means the position of a fresh meat and of a meal are not directly comparable. If you want to compare foods on equal terms, use the guaranteed analysis on a dry matter basis rather than the order of the first few ingredients.

What a "by-product" is

A by-product is what remains of an animal after the main cuts of meat have been taken. AAFCO's definitions describe meat by-products as clean parts other than muscle meat, such as organs (for example lungs, spleen, kidneys and liver), blood and bone, and they explicitly leave out hair, horns, teeth and hooves. Poultry by-products are defined in a similar way and can include parts such as necks, feet and internal organs.

A few points worth keeping in mind:

  • By-products are not one thing. Liver and feet are both by-products, yet they are very different foods. The label does not tell you the mix.
  • "By-product" is not a quality grade. The definition describes which parts are used, not how fresh they are or how they were handled. Quality depends on the supplier and the manufacturer, which a label cannot show.
  • "By-product meal" is simply by-products that have been rendered, the same process as any other meal.

If you prefer to avoid by-products, that is a personal choice you can make; it is not something the label definitions require you to do. Your vet can help you decide what matters for your pet.

The part that matters most for allergies: the species name

For a pet with a suspected or diagnosed food allergy, the more important word on the label is often not "by-product" or "meal" but the name in front of it.

A food can avoid the word "chicken" and still contain chicken under "poultry", "meat and animal derivatives" or "animal digest". We cover this in detail in ambiguous poultry terms and the chicken allergy label check.

The adverse food reaction literature (for example the critically appraised topic by Mueller and colleagues, 2016) reports that the food sources most often involved in dogs and cats are common proteins such as beef, dairy, chicken and fish. That is the reason an unnamed animal source is worth a closer look for an allergic pet, whatever word comes after it.

Fats, digests and flavours: the quieter relatives

The same naming logic applies to ingredients you may not think of as "meat":

  • Animal fat without a species name can come from more than one animal. Rendered fats usually carry very little protein, but how much protein remains depends on processing, which the label does not show.
  • Animal digest is a flavour ingredient made by breaking down animal tissue. It often appears in small amounts, but its source is unnamed unless the label says "chicken digest" or similar.
  • Natural flavour can be of animal or plant origin.

If your vet has asked you to avoid a specific protein completely, for example during an elimination diet, these smaller ingredients matter as much as the ones at the top of the list. Your vet can tell you how strict to be.

How to read these terms, step by step

  1. Find every animal ingredient, not just the first few. Include fats, broths, digests, flavours and supplements such as fish oil.
  2. Check whether each one names its species. "Salmon meal" names it; "fish meal" does not.
  3. Note non-specific terms separately. "Poultry", "meat", "animal" and EU category terms leave the source open.
  4. Compare foods on a dry matter basis, not by which ingredient is listed first.
  5. If something is unclear, ask the manufacturer. Many will tell you which species go into a non-specific ingredient. Write down the answer and the date, because recipes change.
  6. Bring questions about your pet's diet to your vet, especially if your pet has a diagnosed allergy or a medical condition.

Common misunderstandings

  • "Meal means low quality." Meal describes a process (rendering), not a grade.
  • "No by-products means no organs." Some foods list organs by name, for example "chicken liver", which is a by-product under the definition but named individually.
  • "First ingredient = main ingredient." Not necessarily, because of the water in fresh meat.
