Dry matter basis explained: how to compare wet and dry pet food

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

What dry matter is, how to convert a guaranteed analysis step by step, how to estimate carbohydrates honestly, and the mistakes that make comparisons misleading.

A can of cat food says 11% protein. A bag of kibble says 32%. It looks like the kibble has three times the protein. It doesn't, or not necessarily. The can is about 78% water and the kibble about 10%, and protein percentages on a label are calculated on the food as fed, water included. To compare them, you take the water out. What is left is the dry matter.

What dry matter is

Every food is water plus dry matter. Dry matter contains all the nutrients: protein, fat, carbohydrate, fiber, minerals (ash) and vitamins. Water contains none of them, but it is weighed in the percentages on the label.

Dry matter % = 100 − moisture %

A kibble with 10% moisture is 90% dry matter. A wet food with 78% moisture is 22% dry matter.

How to convert a value

Dry matter value = as-fed value ÷ dry matter % × 100

Worked example with two foods:

Wet food (can)Dry food (kibble)
Crude protein (min.)11%32%
Crude fat (min.)5%16%
Crude fiber (max.)1%4%
Moisture (max.)78%10%
Ash (max.)2.5%7.5%
Dry matter22%90%
Protein, dry matter11 ÷ 22 × 100 = 50%32 ÷ 90 × 100 = 35.6%
Fat, dry matter5 ÷ 22 × 100 = 22.7%16 ÷ 90 × 100 = 17.8%

On a dry matter basis, the can in this example has more protein than the kibble, not less. The same conversion applies to fat, fiber and ash.

You can do this with the dry matter calculator, which also estimates carbohydrates when the label allows it.

Estimating carbohydrates

Pet food labels rarely state carbohydrates. They can be estimated by difference, a value often called nitrogen-free extract (NFE):

Carbohydrates (as fed) ≈ 100 − protein − fat − fiber − moisture − ash

For the can above: 100 − 11 − 5 − 1 − 78 − 2.5 = 2.5% as fed, or 2.5 ÷ 22 × 100 ≈ 11% on a dry matter basis. For the kibble: 100 − 32 − 16 − 4 − 10 − 7.5 = 30.5% as fed, ≈ 34% dry matter.

This only works when all five values are on the label. US guaranteed analyses don't require ash, so it is often missing. It is tempting to plug in a "typical" ash value, but ash varies from roughly 5% to more than 10% of dry matter depending on the bone and mineral content of the recipe. An assumed number turns a rough estimate into a false-precise one. When a value is missing, the honest answer is "can't estimate", not zero. Pawfood follows that rule: a missing nutrient is shown as "Not reported", never as 0.

Why the result is always an estimate

  • Minimums and maximums. Protein and fat are minimums; fiber, moisture and ash are maximums. The real values differ, usually in the food's favor for protein and fat. That's why estimates by difference can even come out slightly negative for some wet foods.
  • "Crude" methods. Crude protein is calculated from nitrogen, and crude fiber underestimates total dietary fiber. They are standard but approximate.
  • Typical analysis vs guarantee. Some brands publish a "typical analysis" with average values. It is more precise than the guarantee but not part of the legal label.

Use dry matter values to compare foods with each other, not as exact nutrient contents.

Comparing on energy instead

Dry matter removes water, but foods also differ in fat, and fat carries more than twice the energy of protein or carbohydrate per gram. Veterinary nutritionists often compare nutrients per 1,000 kcal instead, which reflects how much of a nutrient your pet actually eats to meet its energy needs. FEDIAF's nutritional guidelines give minimums both per 100 g dry matter and per 1,000 kcal of metabolizable energy.

If the label gives calories (kcal/kg), the conversion is: nutrient grams per 1,000 kcal = as-fed % × 10,000 ÷ kcal/kg. For a kibble with 32% protein and 3,800 kcal/kg: 32 × 10,000 ÷ 3,800 ≈ 84 g protein per 1,000 kcal.

Common mistakes

  1. Comparing wet and dry as fed. The most common mistake; it always makes wet food look lower in everything.
  2. Reading crude protein as meat content. Pea protein, corn gluten meal and wheat gluten raise crude protein too. The label doesn't split animal and plant protein.
  3. Treating a missing value as zero. Leave the carbohydrate estimate blank if ash isn't stated.
  4. Comparing a complete food with a complementary one. A topper or treat isn't designed to be the whole diet; its analysis isn't comparable to a complete food's. Check the adequacy statement first.
  5. Choosing on one number. Higher protein or lower carbohydrate isn't automatically better for every pet. Needs depend on species, life stage and health, so ask your veterinarian what targets make sense for yours.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher dry matter protein always better?

No. It tells you how foods compare, not what your pet needs. Requirements differ for kittens, senior dogs and pets with health conditions; your veterinarian can tell you what fits.

Why does my carbohydrate estimate come out negative?

Because label values are minimums and maximums, not exact amounts. In very moist foods the rounding can push the difference below zero. Treat that as "very low or not estimable", not as a real negative number.

Do I need the ash value?

For a carbohydrate estimate, yes. For converting protein, fat and fiber to dry matter, you only need moisture.
