Keeping a pet food journal: what to record and how to share it with your vet

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

A practical guide to logging foods, treats, symptoms, stool and weight in a way your vet can use, without reading too much into the patterns yourself.

When your vet asks "what does she eat?" or "when did the itching start?", most of us answer from memory, and memory is not a reliable record. A simple food journal turns vague impressions into dates and details your vet can work with. It is especially useful during an elimination diet, after a food change, or when something comes and goes and you can't tell why.

This guide covers what to write down, how to keep it consistent, and how to share it so it helps rather than overwhelms.

Why a journal helps your vet

The WSAVA Global Nutrition Guidelines describe a diet history as part of a nutritional assessment: what the pet eats, how much, including treats, supplements and food used to give medicine. Owners are the only people who can provide this information.

For suspected food allergies, the Merck Veterinary Manual describes elimination diet trials as strict and lasting several weeks, with your vet judging the result. Over that time, details blur quickly. A journal makes it easier to answer the questions your vet will ask: Was anything else eaten? When did things change? What happened when a food was reintroduced?

A journal is a record of what you observe. It is not a diagnosis, and a pattern in your notes is something to discuss, not a conclusion.

What to record

The food itself

  • Exact product name and variety, not just the brand. "Lamb and rice adult" and "salmon adult" from the same company are different foods.
  • When you started and stopped it. Food periods matter more than individual meals.
  • Lot number and best-before date from the bag or can. If you ever need to report a problem with a product, the U.S. FDA asks for this kind of product information. Taking a photo of the label is the quickest way.
  • Amount per day in the unit you actually use (grams, scoops or cans).

Everything else that is eaten

This is where most journals have gaps (see treats and toppers):

  • treats, chews and dental products
  • toppers, broths and table food
  • supplements and flavoured or chewable medicines
  • anything eaten by accident: another pet's food, something found on a walk

What you observe

Keep observations concrete and describe what you see, not what you think it means:

Instead ofTry
"Allergy flare""Licking front paws most of the evening, both paws red between toes"
"Bad stomach""Vomited once, 2 hours after breakfast, food mostly undigested"
"Better""Scratching noticed twice today, down from most of the day last week"

Useful things to note:

  • Skin and ears: itching, licking, redness, head shaking, odour.
  • Digestion: vomiting, stool consistency, frequency, mucus or blood, gas.
  • Appetite and thirst: skipped meals, eating faster or slower, drinking more.
  • Energy and behaviour: unusual tiredness, restlessness.
  • Weight: regularly, on the same scale, at a similar time of day.

A simple scale, used the same way every time

A rating such as 1 to 5 for itching, or a stool score, makes changes easier to see. Many vets use a stool consistency chart; ask yours which one they prefer. What matters most is that you use the same scale consistently. Your ratings are your own observations, and your vet will interpret them alongside the examination.

Context that may matter

Note other changes without assuming they explain anything: a new shampoo, a flea treatment date, a stay at a kennel, a change in season, a house move, stress, a new pet. Your vet can decide whether they are relevant.

How to keep it consistent

  1. Log at the same moments each day, for example after the evening meal.
  2. Record "nothing unusual" too. A day with no symptoms is information; a blank day is not.
  3. Use one place, so entries from different family members end up together.
  4. Change one thing at a time when your vet agrees to a change. If the food, the treats and the shampoo all change in the same week, no journal can untangle that.
  5. Take photos of skin, stool or vomit when it helps describe what you saw. Note the date.

Signs that shouldn't wait for a pattern

A journal is for slow, recurring questions. If your pet has trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, repeated vomiting, collapse or seizures, contact your vet or an emergency clinic right away; don't wait to see a pattern in the journal.

Sharing it with your vet

A long, unedited log can be hard to use in a short appointment. A few habits help:

  • Send or bring a summary: current food and start date, previous foods with dates, all extras, a short timeline of symptoms, weight trend.
  • Include the list of ingredients you are avoiding, and why (diagnosed, suspected or simple preference).
  • Bring the label, or a clear photo of it, for the current food and treats.
  • Write down your questions before the visit.
  • Ask how your vet would like it: some prefer a printout, some an email, some just the highlights.
  • Follow the plan your vet gives, and record when and how it changed.

Reading your own journal carefully

It is natural to look for connections, and journals do help spot them. Still, two things happening around the same time doesn't mean one explains the other. Allergies, intolerances and other conditions can look similar (see allergy vs intolerance), and symptoms can come and go for many reasons. Use the journal to ask better questions, and let your vet draw the conclusions.
