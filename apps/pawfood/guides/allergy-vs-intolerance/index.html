Food allergy or food intolerance? How they differ in dogs and cats

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Food allergy involves the immune system; intolerance doesn't. Signs overlap, so only a vet-led elimination trial can tell them apart. What to know and note.

When a dog or cat scratches more after a food change, or has loose stools after a new treat, "allergy" is usually the first word that comes up. Sometimes that's right. Often the reaction is something else: an intolerance, a sensitive stomach, or a problem that has nothing to do with food. The difference matters, because it changes how strictly a food needs to be avoided and how a vet will investigate. This guide explains the distinction in plain terms. It isn't a way to work out at home which one your pet has; that takes a veterinarian.

The umbrella term: adverse food reactions

Veterinary sources use adverse food reaction for any abnormal response to something in the diet. It splits into two broad groups:

Food allergy (hypersensitivity)Food intolerance and other non-immune reactions
Immune system involved?Yes. The body reacts to a specific component, usually a protein.No.
ExamplesReaction to beef, dairy, chicken or wheat proteinsDifficulty digesting lactose (a metabolic reaction); reactions to substances with drug-like effects; toxic reactions
Amount that mattersSmall amounts can be enough in a sensitized animalOften depends more on how much was eaten
Typical signsItching (often ears, paws, belly, face), recurring ear or skin infections, digestive signsMostly digestive: gas, soft stool, diarrhea, vomiting
How it's confirmedElimination diet followed by reintroduction, under a vetClinical history, diet trial and ruling out other conditions, under a vet

The Merck Veterinary Manual describes this split: true hypersensitivity on one side, and metabolic, pharmacological and toxic reactions on the other. Lactose intolerance is its example of a metabolic reaction.

Why the signs overlap

Owners can't reliably tell the two apart by looking, for three reasons:

  • Both can show up in the gut. Food allergy isn't only a skin problem. Merck notes that gastrointestinal disease is the most frequently reported condition alongside skin signs in food-allergic dogs and cats.
  • Itching has many explanations. Environmental allergies (atopic dermatitis), fleas, mites and skin infections can all look like a food problem. In a review of the published research, Olivry and Mueller (2017) found that among dogs and cats with itching, food reactions explained a minority of cases; the exact share varied a lot between studies and populations.
  • Timing is misleading. A food allergy isn't always a reaction to a new food. Animals can react to something they've eaten for months or years. Signs can also start at almost any age: in a review of 825 dogs, signs began by 6 or 12 months of age in 22–38% of them, while others started much later (Olivry and Mueller, 2019).

Same food, different mechanism

Dairy is a good example of why the label on the reaction matters more than the label on the food. Milk protein is one of the more commonly reported food allergens in dogs (Mueller and colleagues, 2016). Milk sugar, lactose, is a classic example of intolerance, because many adult animals produce less of the enzyme that digests it.

So a dog that has diarrhea after a bowl of milk may have:

  • an intolerance to lactose (the amount often matters, and some low-lactose dairy, such as hard cheese, may be handled differently), or
  • an allergy to milk protein (small amounts and dairy in any form can matter), or
  • neither, with the diarrhea due to something else.

Only your vet can sort these out, and the answer changes what you need to look for on labels.

Why blood, hair and saliva tests don't settle it

Many tests are marketed to owners for food allergies. Merck reviews serum IgE tests, intradermal tests, hair analysis and saliva tests, and concludes that none of them consistently and accurately diagnoses food allergy. A test may help a vet choose which diet to try, but it doesn't replace the diet trial.

How vets tell them apart

The reference method is an elimination diet followed by a provocation (reintroduction) test:

  1. The pet eats a diet chosen by the vet (a hydrolyzed diet or one built on proteins the pet hasn't eaten before) and nothing else for a set period.
  2. If signs improve, the old food or single ingredients are reintroduced one at a time.
  3. If signs return after a reintroduction and settle again when it's removed, that points to a food allergy to that ingredient.

On timing, a review of published cases (Olivry, Mueller and Prélaud, 2015) found that an 8-week elimination diet was enough for more than 90% of food-allergic dogs and cats to improve; Merck notes that some animals need longer. After a reintroduction, signs returned within 14 days in more than 90% of dogs, and within 7 days in more than 90% of cats (Olivry and Mueller, 2020).

For the label side of choosing a trial diet, see choosing a food for an elimination diet and hydrolyzed protein diets.

What you can do before and during a vet visit

  • Don't start removing foods at random. Changing several things at once makes the result harder to read.
  • Write down what your pet eats. Main food, treats, chews, table scraps, flavored medicines and supplements, with product names. Keep the packaging or photograph the ingredient list.
  • Record what you see, with dates. Scratching, licking paws, ear problems, vomiting, stool changes, appetite. Use the same words each time so changes are easy to spot. Our food journal guide covers what to note.
  • Note what changed. A new food, a new bag of the same food, a new treat, a move, a season.

Contact your vet right away for swelling of the face, difficulty breathing, collapse, repeated vomiting, blood in the stool or a pet that stops eating or drinking. These need prompt attention, whatever is behind them.
