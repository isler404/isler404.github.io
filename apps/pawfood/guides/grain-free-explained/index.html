Grain-free pet food: what the label means and what it doesn't

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Grain-free means no cereal grains; it isn't a low-carb or allergy claim. What replaces grains, and what the FDA said about diet and DCM.

"Grain-free" is one of the most visible claims on pet food packaging. It tells you one thing quite precisely and leaves a lot unsaid. This guide covers what the claim means on a label, what usually takes the place of grains, and what the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has and hasn't said about grain-free diets and a heart condition in dogs called dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM).

What "grain-free" means

A grain-free food has no cereal grains in its ingredient list. That usually means none of these, in any form (whole, flour, bran, gluten, starch or protein concentrate):

  • wheat and its relatives such as spelt
  • corn (maize)
  • rice, including brown rice and brewers rice
  • barley, oats, rye, sorghum and millet

Pseudocereals such as quinoa and buckwheat are botanically not grains, and some grain-free recipes include them. If you are avoiding something specific, read the ingredient list rather than relying on the front-of-pack claim.

"Grain-free" describes what was left out. It is not a nutritional category. A grain-free food sold as complete and balanced has to meet the same nutrient profiles (AAFCO in the US, FEDIAF guidelines in Europe) as any other complete food for that species and life stage. The nutritional adequacy statement tells you more about a food's purpose than the grain-free claim does.

What "grain-free" does not mean

It doesn't mean carbohydrate-free. Dry food needs starch to form kibble. In grain-free recipes the starch usually comes from:

  • legumes (pulses): peas, lentils, chickpeas, beans
  • tubers and roots: potato, sweet potato, tapioca

A grain-free kibble can contain as much starch as a grain-inclusive one. Labels rarely state carbohydrate. You can estimate it only when protein, fat, fiber, moisture and ash are all printed; if one is missing, the estimate isn't possible.

It doesn't mean lower in plant protein. Legume fractions such as pea protein are concentrated plant proteins. They raise the crude protein figure on the label, but that figure doesn't say how much protein comes from animal and how much from plant sources.

It doesn't settle allergy questions. Grains are not the most commonly reported food allergens in dogs and cats. In a 2016 review of published cases (Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud), beef, dairy and chicken were reported more often than wheat in dogs, and beef and fish were the most often reported in cats; corn and rice were reported less often. Any protein in a food, grain-free or not, can be a problem for an individual animal. See our guides on allergens in dogs and allergens in cats.

It doesn't mean gluten-free in every sense. Without wheat, barley and rye, a grain-free recipe has no gluten from its ingredients. The ingredient list can't tell you whether shared production lines were used, so it can't rule out traces.

It doesn't mean better or worse. The presence or absence of grains doesn't, on its own, tell you whether a food suits your pet. WSAVA's guidance on choosing a food points owners to the manufacturer's nutrition expertise and quality control rather than to individual ingredient claims.

Grain-free diets and DCM: what the FDA said

DCM is a disease of the heart muscle. Some breeds have a known genetic predisposition. In July 2018 the FDA announced it was looking into reports of DCM in dogs, including breeds not typically predisposed, that had been eating certain diets. Many of those diets were labeled grain-free and had peas, lentils, other pulses or potatoes high in the ingredient list.

The key points from the FDA's published updates:

  • The reports. Between January 1, 2014 and April 30, 2019, the FDA received 524 reports of DCM (515 involving dogs, 9 involving cats). In its 2019 update, it noted that 93% of the reported products contained peas and/or lentils and about 42% contained potatoes or sweet potatoes.
  • What that does and doesn't show. These are frequencies in voluntary reports. The FDA has said that adverse event reports alone do not supply sufficient data to establish a causal relationship with the products reported.
  • Complexity. The FDA has described the possible link between diet and DCM as a complex scientific issue that may involve multiple factors. It has not identified a single ingredient or nutrient as responsible, and it looked at, among other things, whether taurine metabolism plays a role.
  • No recall or ban. The FDA has not asked manufacturers to stop selling grain-free foods.
  • Current status. In its December 2022 update, the FDA said it does not intend to release further public updates until there is meaningful new scientific information to share.

Research has continued outside the FDA. A 2018 commentary in JAVMA by veterinary cardiologists and nutritionists described the cases and grouped the diets as "BEG" (boutique companies, exotic ingredients, or grain-free), noting that not all of the diets involved were grain-free. A 2021 laboratory study in Scientific Reports (Smith and colleagues) compared the chemical make-up of diets associated with DCM and other diets; peas showed the strongest association with the compounds that differed. The authors described their findings as associations, not proof that any ingredient leads to DCM.

In short: there is an association under study, it isn't explained, and no regulator has concluded that grain-free foods as a group are responsible.

What this means for you

  • If your dog eats a grain-free food and is doing well, there's no need to panic or switch overnight. Bring it up at your next vet visit, especially for breeds with a known predisposition to heart disease.
  • If you notice signs such as reduced energy, coughing, faster or harder breathing at rest, or fainting, contact your vet promptly, whatever the dog eats.
  • If you choose to change foods, change gradually. See switching foods.
  • If your pet has a vet-diagnosed food allergy, choose by the specific ingredients your vet asked you to avoid, not by the grain-free claim.
  • Cats appeared in a small number of reports. Cats have their own taurine requirements; see cats and taurine.
