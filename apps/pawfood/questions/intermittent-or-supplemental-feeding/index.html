What does 'for intermittent or supplemental feeding only' mean?

Last updated 12 November 2026 · By Pawfood

The product isn't nutritionally complete, so it isn't meant as the main diet. Seen on treats, toppers and some wet foods; EU: 'complementary feed'.

  2. Why it matters
  3. How much is "supplemental"?
  4. For allergies
  5. How Pawfood shows this

It means the product doesn't meet the nutrient levels for a complete diet, so it isn't meant to be fed on its own as your pet's main food. You'll see it on treats, toppers, broths and some wet foods. In the EU, the equivalent wording is complementary feed. The opposite is "complete and balanced" (US) or complete feed (EU).

Where you'll see it

  • Treats, chews and biscuits.
  • Toppers, broths, gravies and mixers.
  • Some wet foods, especially recipes that are mainly one meat or fish, such as tuna in broth for cats.
  • Some "gourmet" or specialty products.

The wording is part of the nutritional adequacy statement. If a product has no adequacy statement at all, it is usually a treat or complementary product.

Why it matters

A food that's mostly meat or fish may lack calcium, vitamins or other nutrients. Fed occasionally next to a complete food, that's fine. Fed as the main diet over weeks or months, it can lead to deficiencies. Cats are especially dependent on complete foods for nutrients such as taurine.

How much is "supplemental"?

Labels rarely say. WSAVA's nutrition guidelines suggest that treats and extras should make up only a small share of daily calories; ask your vet what fits your pet, especially if you use toppers every day.

For allergies

Toppers and broths often carry common proteins such as chicken or fish. During an elimination diet, they usually need to be left out. See treats and toppers.
