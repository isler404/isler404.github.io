How do I calculate the carbohydrates in pet food?

Last updated 12 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Subtract protein, fat, fiber, moisture and ash from 100. It only works when all five are on the label, and the result is an estimate.

On this page

Most pet food labels don't list carbohydrates, but you can estimate them "by difference": 100 minus crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, moisture and ash. Then convert to a dry matter basis to compare foods. The estimate only works when all five values are on the label; if ash is missing, the honest answer is that you can't calculate it.

The formula

Carbohydrates (as fed) ≈ 100 − protein − fat − fiber − moisture − ash

Carbohydrates (dry matter) ≈ carbohydrates as fed ÷ (100 − moisture) × 100

This value is sometimes called nitrogen-free extract (NFE).

Example

A kibble lists: protein 30%, fat 16%, fiber 4%, moisture 10%, ash 8%.

  • As fed: 100 − 30 − 16 − 4 − 10 − 8 = 32%
  • Dry matter: 32 ÷ 90 × 100 ≈ 36%

The numbers are an illustration, not typical for any product.

Why it's only an estimate

  • Minimums and maximums. Protein and fat are minimums, so the true values are usually higher; fiber, moisture and ash are maximums. The estimate often comes out a bit high, and in very moist foods it can even turn negative.
  • Crude fiber undercounts fiber. Some fiber ends up counted as carbohydrate.
  • Ash is often missing. US labels don't have to list it. Assuming a "typical" value creates false precision, because ash varies widely with bone and mineral content.

Using the result

Carbohydrate levels are useful for comparing foods, but a lower number isn't automatically better. Carbohydrate needs depend on the species, life stage, weight and health of your pet, and some conditions call for specific diets. Discuss targets with your vet. Grain-free foods aren't necessarily lower in carbohydrate; peas, potatoes and tapioca are carbohydrate sources too (grain-free explained).
