What does 'crude protein' mean on dog food?

Last updated 12 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Crude protein is total protein estimated from nitrogen content. 'Crude' refers to the lab method, not quality, and it includes plant and animal protein.

Crude protein is the food's total protein, estimated by measuring its nitrogen content and multiplying by a standard factor. "Crude" refers to that lab method, not to poor quality. On a US label it is a minimum, so the real amount is at least that much. It doesn't tell you where the protein comes from or how digestible it is.

Why "crude"

Protein contains nitrogen in a fairly predictable share. Labs measure nitrogen and convert it to protein, which is quick and standard but approximate: nitrogen from non-protein sources is counted too. The same word, "crude", is used for fat, fiber and ash, again describing the method.

What it doesn't tell you

  • Animal or plant. Pea protein, potato protein, corn gluten meal, wheat gluten and soybean meal all raise crude protein. The label doesn't split protein by source. Read the ingredient list to see what's contributing.
  • Digestibility and amino acids. Two foods with the same crude protein can differ in how much the animal absorbs and in essential amino acids. The label doesn't show this.
  • The exact amount. It is a minimum; the actual value is usually somewhat higher.

How to compare foods

Crude protein is given as fed, including water. To compare a wet food with a kibble, or two kibbles with different moisture, convert to dry matter: divide by (100 − moisture) and multiply by 100. Nutritionists also compare protein per 1,000 kcal, because foods differ in energy.

Is more protein better?

Not automatically. Protein needs depend on species, life stage, activity and health. Cats need more protein than dogs, growing animals more than adults, and some medical conditions call for a different amount. Your vet can tell you what range fits your pet.

For allergies

Crude protein says nothing about allergens. Food allergies are reactions to specific proteins, so what matters is which animals and plants are on the ingredient list, not the percentage.
