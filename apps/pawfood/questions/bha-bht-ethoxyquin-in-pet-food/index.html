What are BHA, BHT and ethoxyquin in pet food?

Last updated 12 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Synthetic antioxidants that keep fats from going rancid. BHA and BHT are permitted within limits; the EU no longer authorizes ethoxyquin in feed.

BHA (butylated hydroxyanisole), BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene) and ethoxyquin are synthetic antioxidants that slow fats from going rancid. BHA and BHT are permitted in pet food within set conditions and limits. Ethoxyquin was used widely in feed, including fish meal, but the EU denied its authorization as a feed additive in 2022. Whether to avoid them is a personal choice; health concerns about a specific pet belong with your vet.

Why they're used

Fats oxidize over time, which produces off-flavors and breaks down nutrients. Synthetic antioxidants work at low levels and stay stable through cooking and storage, which is why they've been used in human food, feed and cosmetics for decades.

BHA and BHT

Regulators set conditions of use and maximum levels for BHA and BHT, and these aren't identical everywhere. They remain the subject of debate: some owners avoid them on principle, others accept them because they're authorized and used at low levels. A label won't settle that question for you.

Ethoxyquin

In the EU, Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/1375 denied the authorization of ethoxyquin as a feed additive, after the EU food authority couldn't conclude on its use because data were missing. Transition periods allowed existing stocks to be used up. A denial reflects missing evidence in an assessment, not proof of harm at the levels used. In other regions, ethoxyquin may still be permitted under specific conditions.

Where to look on the label

  • In brackets after fats and oils: "animal fat (preserved with BHA)".
  • In the additives section of EU labels, sometimes as "antioxidants" with or without the specific name.
  • Not always on the label: preservatives added to a raw material, such as fish meal, before it reaches the pet food maker may not appear.

Alternatives

Natural antioxidants such as mixed tocopherols, rosemary extract and citric acid do the same job, less strongly. Foods using them may have shorter shelf lives once opened.
