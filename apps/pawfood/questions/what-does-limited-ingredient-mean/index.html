What does 'limited ingredient' mean on pet food?

Last updated 12 November 2026 · By Pawfood

It's a marketing term for a recipe with fewer ingredients, often one main protein. It has no set legal definition and isn't a medical claim.

"Limited ingredient" describes a recipe with a shorter ingredient list, usually built around one animal protein and one or two carbohydrate sources. It is a marketing term without a set legal definition in AAFCO's label rules or EU feed law, so there's no fixed number of ingredients behind it. It doesn't mean the food is suitable for a pet with food allergies.

Why people choose them

Fewer ingredients make it easier to avoid a specific protein and to see what your pet is eating. Many owners use them when a vet has asked them to avoid one or two foods.

What to check

  • Count the proteins yourself. A "limited ingredient duck" recipe can still list chicken fat, fish oil, egg or "natural flavor".
  • Look past the first lines. Vitamins, minerals and flavorings sit at the end, and some carry animal protein (for example, flavors and digests).
  • Check the adequacy statement. A limited recipe still needs to be complete and balanced for your pet's life stage.

Limited ingredient foods and elimination trials

Studies have found that many commercial pet foods contain ingredients not declared on the label. In a review of such studies, undeclared ingredients were detected in 33% to 83% of foods sold as "novel" or "limited" ingredient diets, depending on the study (Olivry and Mueller 2018). Whether those traces lead to reactions isn't known. For a strict elimination trial, vets often prefer a veterinary diet or a home-prepared recipe; ask yours which fits.

Limited ingredient vs novel protein vs hydrolysed

  • Limited ingredient: fewer ingredients; the protein may or may not be new to your pet.
  • Novel protein: a protein your pet has never eaten.
  • Hydrolysed: proteins broken into small pieces; see hydrolyzed protein diets.
