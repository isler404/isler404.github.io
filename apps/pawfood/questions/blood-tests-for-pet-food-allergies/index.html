Can a blood, hair or saliva test diagnose food allergies in dogs and cats?

Last updated 12 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Not reliably. Blood tests for food allergy proved inaccurate in reviews, and hair and saliva tests are unproven. A vet-led elimination diet is the standard.

Not reliably. A review of the evidence found that blood tests for food-specific antibodies (IgE and IgG) had low repeatability and variable accuracy in dogs, and low accuracy in cats (Mueller and Olivry 2017). Hair and saliva tests sold to owners haven't been shown to diagnose food allergy. The standard method remains an elimination diet followed by a food challenge, guided by your vet.

What the review found

Mueller and Olivry (2017) evaluated published studies on tests for adverse food reactions in dogs and cats:

  • Serum IgE and IgG tests: low repeatability; highly variable accuracy in dogs; low accuracy in cats.
  • Lymphocyte proliferation tests: more accurate, but complex; currently a research tool.
  • Patch tests: negative results were highly predictive in dogs and may help choose ingredients for an elimination diet in selected cases.
  • Other tests (fecal IgE, endoscopic tests): less useful or studied only in small numbers.

The authors concluded that an elimination diet with subsequent food challenges is still the best way to identify food reactions.

Why tests can mislead

A test can show antibodies to a food your pet eats without trouble, or miss the food that matters. Changing diet based on a test result can delay finding the real answer, or limit your pet's diet unnecessarily.

What an elimination diet involves

Your vet chooses a diet based on what your pet has eaten before, often a novel protein or a hydrolysed diet. Your pet eats only that for at least 8 weeks in most cases (Olivry, Mueller and Prélaud 2015). If signs improve, a food challenge reintroduces the old food to see whether they return.
