Dairy allergy in dogs and cats: what to check on the label

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

If your vet has confirmed a dairy allergy in your dog or cat, or you simply avoid dairy, the ingredients list is where you check. This page lists the label names that always mean dairy, the vaguer terms that might, and how Pawfood flags both.

What counts as dairy

Dairy covers milk from cows, goats, sheep and yaks and everything made from it: milk powder, whey, casein, milk protein concentrate, cheese, yogurt, kefir, buttermilk, butter, cream, colostrum and lactoferrin. A milk protein allergy is different from lactose intolerance; lactose-free milk still carries milk protein.

Terms that might mean dairy

These wordings can contain dairy, but the label doesn't say exactly what they come from: dried fermentation products, natural flavor, lactose, animal protein, galactooligosaccharides, bread.

Lactose and galactooligosaccharides are milk sugars, usually with little protein left, but some may remain. Dried fermentation products can be grown on milk. Animal protein and natural flavor don't name their source, and bread may contain milk.

Fats, oils and hydrolyzed forms: less protein, not none

Food allergies are reactions to proteins (Merck Veterinary Manual), and processing changes how much intact protein an ingredient carries. Pawfood rates these as reduced carryover: hydrolyzed whey protein, butter. Reduced is not zero. Fats and oils can hold traces of protein, and hydrolysis breaks proteins into smaller pieces without removing every reactive fragment. Whether your pet can have these forms is a question for your vet.

How often it's reported in dogs and cats

Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud (2016) pooled published studies of dogs and cats with food allergy. The reported allergen sources included dairy in about 17% of dogs. For cats, dairy is among the less often reported sources. Many animals react to more than one food, so the figures overlap. They describe those studies, not your own pet's odds.

Label checklist for dairy

  • Read the full ingredients list, not the front of the pack. Under AAFCO naming rules a "beef dinner" needs only 25% beef, and "with" or "flavor" names need far less.
  • Check every name in the table above, including fats, broths, digests and plasma.
  • Treat unnamed wordings ("meat", "animal fat", "natural flavor") as unknown and ask the manufacturer what they are made from.
  • Read inside parentheses and the vitamin and mineral part of the list.
  • Lactose-free milk and goat milk are still dairy.
  • Look for whey and casein in wet foods, gravies and senior or kitten formulas.
  • Recheck each new bag and each product separately: recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.

Treats, toppers and chews

Cheese treats, yak cheese chews, yogurt drops, goat milk toppers, cat milk and frozen dairy treats are common sources. Check treats, chews, toppers and flavored supplements like food, and ask your vet before giving any during an elimination diet.

How Pawfood flags dairy

When dairy is on a pet's avoid list, Pawfood compares each ingredient it recognizes with the lists above:

  • A listed dairy ingredient: Match found if you saved dairy as a vet-confirmed allergy or as something you just avoid; Worth a closer look if you saved it as a suspected sensitivity.
  • A reduced-carryover form (fat, oil, hydrolyzed, flavor): still a match for a vet-confirmed allergy, with a note that it carries less protein.
  • A term that might mean dairy: Worth a closer look, because the label doesn't name the source.
  • No recorded match: nothing on the listed ingredients matched your records. It doesn't mean the food is allergen-free; labels can be incomplete and recipes change.

Words Pawfood doesn't recognize are shown as unrecognized, so check those against the label photo yourself.

Talk to your vet

Pawfood explains labels; it doesn't diagnose, treat or prescribe diets. Food allergy is usually confirmed with an elimination diet and food challenge run by a vet, so talk to yours before removing dairy or switching foods. If your pet has trouble breathing, facial swelling, repeated vomiting or collapses, contact a vet or emergency clinic right away.
