Corn (maize) on pet food labels — names, by-products and what to check

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

What the corn family covers

Corn, also called maize, is a cereal grain used in many dry foods. It can provide starch for kibble, protein (corn gluten meal, corn protein concentrate), fiber (corn bran) and fat (corn oil). Milling corn produces several co-products, and each has its own label name. Corn is also part of the broader cereal grains family. It is not a gluten grain: "corn gluten" is a corn protein, not wheat gluten.

Hominy feed and corn gluten feed are milling co-products. "Maize" is the usual word on European labels, so "maize gluten" and "maize starch" are corn too.

Possible or unclear ingredients

These terms may or may not come from corn. Pawfood shows them as a possible match:

maltodextrin, modified starch, natural flavor, hydrolyzed vegetable protein, vegetable protein, gravy, vegetable oil, corn syrup, dried distillers grains, cereals, flour, vegetable derivatives.

Maltodextrin, modified starch and corn syrup are refined from starch, and starch is very often corn-based. "Vegetable oil" and "vegetable protein" can come from several plants. Dried distillers grains are left over from ethanol production and are frequently, but not always, corn. "Cereals", "flour" and "vegetable derivatives" are collective names that do not list the plants. Gravies and natural flavors may use corn-based thickeners. The manufacturer can tell you the source.

Meal, gluten meal, starch, oil: why the form matters

Corn gluten meal and corn protein concentrate are concentrated corn protein. Ground corn, corn meal, germ meal and the milling co-products keep much of it. Corn starch and corn oil are refined fractions, so very little corn protein is expected in them; Pawfood marks their protein carryover as minimal.

Minimal is not the same as none. Whether refined corn ingredients matter for your pet is a question for your vet.

How often corn is reported

In a 2016 review of published studies (Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud), corn was reported in 13 of 297 dogs with adverse food reactions (4%), well behind beef, dairy, chicken and wheat. Corn also appears in the cat data, less often than beef, fish or chicken. These figures show what was reported in studies, not how likely your own pet is to react.

Label checklist

  • Look for both "corn" and "maize", including maize gluten and maize starch.
  • Remember that corn gluten meal is corn, even in a food that says "no wheat".
  • Treat maltodextrin, modified starch, corn syrup, "vegetable oil" and distillers grains as unclear until you know the source.
  • Check coatings, gravies and jelly in wet food.
  • "Grain-free" refers to the recipe only; it says nothing about shared production lines.
  • Re-check when the packaging or recipe changes.

Treats and toppers

Corn starch, corn syrup and corn meal are common in chews, jerky-style treats, biscuits and dental sticks. Popcorn and leftover corn from the table are easy to forget. If corn is on your pet's list, check every treat and topper as carefully as the main food.

Talk to your vet

Skin, ear or digestive problems have many possible causes, and a food link can only be worked out with your veterinarian, usually through an elimination diet. Ask them before switching food. If your pet has trouble breathing, swelling or collapses, contact a vet or an emergency clinic right away.

How Pawfood flags corn

When you add corn, or cereal grains, to a pet's profile:

  • Every ingredient in the table above is shown as a match. A vet-confirmed allergy or an "avoid" entry is a match; a suspected sensitivity is shown for review.
  • For a vet-confirmed entry, corn starch and corn oil also get a note about lower protein carryover. The match stays.
  • Unclear terms such as maltodextrin or "vegetable oil" appear as a possible match.

"No recorded match" doesn't mean allergen-free. Labels can be incomplete, recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.
