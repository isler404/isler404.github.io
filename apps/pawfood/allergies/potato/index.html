Potato on pet food labels — dried potato, starch, protein and fiber

What the potato family covers

Potato is a starchy tuber, not a grain, and is a common carbohydrate in grain-free dry foods. It is used whole (usually dried or dehydrated), and in fractions: potato starch, potato protein and potato fiber or pulp. In Pawfood's knowledge base potato is its own family. Sweet potato is a different plant and has a separate family, so it is not matched when you save potato.

"Potato pulp" is the fibrous part left after starch extraction and counts as potato fiber.

Possible or unclear ingredients

Pawfood shows these as a possible match for potato:

maltodextrin, modified starch, vegetable protein, mixed vegetables, vegetable derivatives.

Maltodextrin and modified starch can be made from potato, corn, wheat, rice or tapioca. "Vegetable protein", "mixed vegetables" and "vegetable derivatives" are collective names that may include potato without listing it. The manufacturer can tell you the source.

Whole potato, starch, protein, fiber: why the form matters

Dried potato keeps the tuber's own protein, and potato protein is a concentrate of it. Potato fiber carries less. Potato starch is a refined fraction with very little protein expected, so Pawfood marks its carryover as minimal. Minimal is not none, and your vet is the right person to decide whether it matters for your pet.

How often potato is reported

The 2016 review of published studies by Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud does not list potato among the commonly reported food allergen sources in dogs or cats. That reflects the studies available; it does not rule out a reaction in an individual pet.

Separately, the U.S. FDA has looked into reports of a heart condition (dilated cardiomyopathy) in dogs, many of whom ate foods with peas, lentils, other legumes or potatoes high on the ingredient list. The FDA has not concluded that these ingredients are the cause. If you have questions about your dog's diet and heart health, talk to your vet.

Label checklist

  • Search for potato, dried or dehydrated potato, potato starch, potato protein, potato fiber and potato pulp.
  • Note where potato sits in the list: ingredients are listed by weight, so a potato near the top is a main ingredient.
  • Treat maltodextrin, modified starch, "vegetable protein" and "mixed vegetables" as unclear until you know the source.
  • Do not confuse potato with sweet potato; they are separate families.
  • Re-check after recipe or packaging changes.

Treats and toppers

Potato starch and dried potato are common in grain-free treats, soft chews and dental sticks. Chips, fries and mashed potato from the table are easy extras to forget. If potato is on your pet's list, check every treat and topper as well.

Talk to your vet

Skin and digestive signs have many causes. Only your veterinarian can work out whether potato is involved, usually through an elimination diet, and advise you on choosing a food. If your pet has trouble breathing, swelling or collapses, contact a vet or an emergency clinic right away.

How Pawfood flags potato

When you add potato to a pet's profile:

  • Potato, potato protein, potato starch and potato fiber are shown as a match. A vet-confirmed allergy or an "avoid" entry is a match; a suspected sensitivity is shown for review.
  • For a vet-confirmed entry, potato starch and potato fiber also get a note about lower protein carryover. The match stays.
  • Unclear starches and vegetable terms appear as a possible match.

"No recorded match" doesn't mean allergen-free. Labels can be incomplete, recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.
