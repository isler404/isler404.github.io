Fish allergy in dogs and cats: salmon, whitefish, tuna and more on the label

If your vet has confirmed a fish allergy in your dog or cat, or you simply avoid fish, the ingredients list is where you check. This page lists the label names that always mean fish, the vaguer terms that might, and how Pawfood flags both.

What counts as fish

Fish in Pawfood covers all finfish. Each species (salmon, tuna, sardine and so on) is its own sub-family, and cod, pollock, haddock, hake and similar species also sit inside a whitefish group. So you can save "fish" as a whole, or one species such as salmon. Shellfish (krill, shrimp, mussel) is a separate family.

Terms that might mean fish

These wordings can contain fish, but the label doesn't say exactly what they come from: chondroitin sulfate, omega-3 fatty acids, natural flavor, gravy, seafood, animal protein, hydrolyzed animal protein, animal fat, animal digest, gelatin, collagen, broth.

Seafood can mean fish or shellfish, and omega-3 fatty acids may come from fish, krill or algae. Animal protein, animal fat, gelatin and broth don't name their source, and "fish" or "ocean fish" don't name a species.

Fats, oils and hydrolyzed forms: less protein, not none

Food allergies are reactions to proteins (Merck Veterinary Manual), and processing changes how much intact protein an ingredient carries. Pawfood rates these as reduced carryover: fish oil, hydrolyzed fish protein, natural fish flavor, fish gelatin, salmon oil, hydrolyzed salmon protein, natural salmon flavor, trout oil, whitefish oil, hydrolyzed whitefish protein, tuna oil, natural tuna flavor, hydrolyzed tuna protein, sardine oil, herring oil, anchovy oil, menhaden oil, cod liver oil, pollock oil, mackerel oil. Reduced is not zero. Fats and oils can hold traces of protein, and hydrolysis breaks proteins into smaller pieces without removing every reactive fragment. Whether your pet can have these forms is a question for your vet.

How often it's reported in dogs and cats

Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud (2016) pooled published studies of dogs and cats with food allergy. The reported allergen sources included fish in about 2% of dogs and about 17% of cats. Many animals react to more than one food, so the figures overlap. They describe those studies, not your own pet's odds.

Label checklist for fish

  • Read the full ingredients list, not the front of the pack. Under AAFCO naming rules a "beef dinner" needs only 25% beef, and "with" or "flavor" names need far less.
  • Check every name in the table above, including fats, broths, digests and plasma.
  • Treat unnamed wordings ("meat", "animal fat", "natural flavor") as unknown and ask the manufacturer what they are made from.
  • Read inside parentheses and the vitamin and mineral part of the list.
  • Fish oil, salmon oil and cod liver oil turn up in recipes led by meat.
  • Recheck each new bag and each product separately: recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.

Treats, toppers and chews

Fish skins, dried sprats, tuna toppers and salmon oil are common; cat treats often use fish broth. Check treats, chews, toppers and flavored supplements like food, and ask your vet before giving any during an elimination diet.

How Pawfood flags fish

When fish is on a pet's avoid list, Pawfood compares each ingredient it recognizes with the lists above:

  • A listed fish ingredient: Match found if you saved fish as a vet-confirmed allergy or as something you just avoid; Worth a closer look if you saved it as a suspected sensitivity.
  • A reduced-carryover form (fat, oil, hydrolyzed, flavor): still a match for a vet-confirmed allergy, with a note that it carries less protein.
  • A term that might mean fish: Worth a closer look, because the label doesn't name the source.
  • No recorded match: nothing on the listed ingredients matched your records. It doesn't mean the food is allergen-free; labels can be incomplete and recipes change.

Words Pawfood doesn't recognize are shown as unrecognized, so check those against the label photo yourself.

Talk to your vet

Pawfood explains labels; it doesn't diagnose, treat or prescribe diets. Food allergy is usually confirmed with an elimination diet and food challenge run by a vet, so talk to yours before removing fish or switching foods. If your pet has trouble breathing, facial swelling, repeated vomiting or collapses, contact a vet or emergency clinic right away.
