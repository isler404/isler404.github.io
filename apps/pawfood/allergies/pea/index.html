Peas on pet food labels — pea protein, starch, fiber and split names

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

Peas appear on pet food labels as whole peas, pea protein, starch and fiber, often several times in one list. See every name and how Pawfood flags them.

What the pea family covers

Peas are legumes. Dried field peas are widely used in grain-free dry foods, both as a carbohydrate and, through pea protein, as a plant protein source. Garden peas (green peas) appear more often in wet foods and toppers. Peas can also be split into fractions: pea protein, pea starch and pea fiber. In Pawfood's knowledge base peas belong to the wider legumes family, together with lentils, chickpeas, beans, soy, peanuts and lupin.

Because ingredients are listed by weight, a recipe can list "peas", "pea protein" and "pea fiber" separately. Together they may add up to more than any single ingredient shown above them, so it helps to look at the whole list.

Possible or unclear ingredients

Pawfood shows these as a possible match for peas:

modified starch, hydrolyzed vegetable protein, vegetable protein, legumes, mixed vegetables.

Modified starch is sometimes made from peas, though corn, wheat, potato and tapioca are also common. "Vegetable protein" and "hydrolyzed vegetable protein" do not name the plant. "Legumes" and "mixed vegetables" are collective names that often include peas. The manufacturer can tell you what is inside.

Whole peas, protein, starch, fiber: why the form matters

Whole dried peas and garden peas keep their own protein. Pea protein is a concentrate. Hydrolyzed pea protein has been broken into smaller pieces, which lowers its allergen potential without removing it. Pea starch and pea fiber carry less protein than whole peas, so Pawfood marks their carryover as reduced, not minimal: these fractions are less refined than, for example, corn starch. Reduced still means some protein may be present.

How often peas are reported

The 2016 review of published studies by Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud does not list peas among the commonly reported food allergen sources in dogs or cats. That reflects the studies available and tells you little about any one pet.

The U.S. FDA has also looked into reports of a heart condition (dilated cardiomyopathy) in dogs, many of whom ate foods with peas, lentils, other legumes or potatoes high on the ingredient list. The FDA has not established that these ingredients are the cause. Questions about your dog's diet and heart health belong with your vet.

Label checklist

  • Search for peas, pea protein, pea starch, pea fiber (or "pea bran") and garden or green peas.
  • Add up split pea ingredients when judging how much pea a recipe contains.
  • Treat "legumes", "mixed vegetables", "vegetable protein" and modified starch as unclear until the source is known.
  • If your vet suggested avoiding all legumes, use the legumes family instead of peas alone.
  • Re-check after recipe or packaging changes.

Treats and toppers

Pea protein and pea flour are common in grain-free biscuits, soft chews and plant-based treats, and green peas often appear in toppers and wet-food "stews". If peas are on your pet's list, check every extra too.

Talk to your vet

Skin and digestive signs have many possible causes. Only your veterinarian can work out whether peas play a part, usually through an elimination diet, and help you choose a food. If your pet has trouble breathing, swelling or collapses, contact a vet or an emergency clinic right away.

How Pawfood flags peas

When you add peas, or legumes, to a pet's profile:

  • Every ingredient in the table above is shown as a match. A vet-confirmed allergy or an "avoid" entry is a match; a suspected sensitivity is shown for review.
  • For a vet-confirmed entry, pea starch, pea fiber and hydrolyzed pea protein also get a note about lower protein carryover. The match stays.
  • Unclear terms appear as a possible match.

"No recorded match" doesn't mean allergen-free. Labels can be incomplete, recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.
