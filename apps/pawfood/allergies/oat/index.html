Oats on pet food labels — oatmeal, oat bran, oat fiber and cat grass

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

What the oat family covers

Oats are a cereal grain used in pet food as a carbohydrate and fiber source, often as ground or rolled oats (oatmeal) or as oat groats. Milling gives two more ingredients: oat bran, the outer layer, and oat fiber, made mostly from the hulls. Oat grass, the young leaves of the oat plant, is sold as "cat grass" and also appears in some foods and supplements. Oats belong to the wider cereal grains family.

"Oatmeal" on a pet food label means ground oats, not a cooked porridge. "Groats" are whole oat kernels with the hull removed.

Possible or unclear ingredients

Pawfood shows these as a possible match for oats:

beta-glucans, cereals, flour.

Beta-glucans are soluble fibers that can be extracted from oats, barley or yeast. "Cereals" and "flour" are collective names that do not say which grain was used. Ask the manufacturer if you need to know.

Oats and gluten

Oats do not contain wheat gluten. They are, however, often grown in rotation with wheat and barley and handled in the same mills, so some contact with gluten grains is possible. For that reason Pawfood lists oats as a possible member of the gluten grains family rather than a definite one. If your pet avoids gluten grains, your vet can tell you whether oats belong on the list too.

Whole oats, bran, fiber, grass: why the form matters

Rolled or ground oats and oat bran keep the grain's protein. Oat fiber is made mainly from hulls and carries very little protein, so Pawfood marks its carryover as minimal. Oat grass is a leaf picked before the grain forms, so it also carries little grain protein. Minimal is not the same as none, and the label cannot tell you how much is present.

How often oats are reported

The 2016 review of published studies by Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud does not place oats among the commonly reported food sources in dogs or cats. That reflects what was studied and reported; it does not mean oats cannot matter for an individual pet.

Label checklist

  • Search for oats, oatmeal, groats, oat flour, oat bran and oat fiber.
  • Check the fiber part of the list: oat fiber is often added to weight-control and hairball formulas.
  • Treat beta-glucans, "cereals" and "flour" as unclear until the source is known.
  • If gluten grains are on your pet's list, ask your vet how to handle oats.
  • "Grain-free" refers only to the recipe.
  • Re-check after recipe or packaging changes.

Treats and toppers

Oat flour and oats are common in baked biscuits and "homestyle" treats. Cat grass kits and grass-based supplements are oat or wheat grass, so read the packet. Count every extra during an elimination diet.

Talk to your vet

Digestive and skin problems have many causes, and only your veterinarian can work out whether oats are involved, usually through an elimination diet. If your pet has trouble breathing, swelling or collapses, contact a vet or an emergency clinic right away.

How Pawfood flags oats

When you add oats or cereal grains to a pet's profile:

  • Oats, oat bran, oat fiber and oat grass are shown as a match. A vet-confirmed allergy or an "avoid" entry is a match; a suspected sensitivity is shown for review.
  • For a vet-confirmed entry, oat fiber and oat grass also get a note about lower protein carryover. The match stays.
  • Beta-glucans and collective grain names appear as a possible match.
  • If you saved gluten grains instead, oats appear as a possible match too.

"No recorded match" doesn't mean allergen-free. Labels can be incomplete, recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.
