Chicken allergy in dogs and cats: what to check on the label

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

If your vet has confirmed a chicken allergy in your dog or cat, or you simply avoid chicken, the ingredients list is where you check. This page lists the label names that always mean chicken, the vaguer terms that might, and how Pawfood flags both.

What counts as chicken

Chicken covers the meat, organs, skin, cartilage and fat of the domestic chicken and everything made from them: meals, broths, digests, plasma and hydrolysates. Chicken is used very widely in pet food, so it often appears in recipes named after another protein. Eggs are a separate family in Pawfood (egg).

Terms that might mean chicken

These wordings can contain chicken, but the label doesn't say exactly what they come from: poultry, poultry meal, poultry by-product meal, poultry by-products, poultry fat, hydrolyzed poultry protein, hydrolyzed poultry liver, poultry liver, poultry heart, poultry gizzards, poultry broth, poultry digest, natural poultry flavor, dried poultry, dehydrated poultry protein, poultry giblets, dried poultry plasma, chondroitin sulfate, hyaluronic acid, natural flavor, gravy, meat, meat and animal derivatives, meat by-product meal, animal protein, hydrolyzed animal protein, animal fat, animal digest, liver, animal plasma, blood meal, gelatin, collagen, bone broth, broth, bone meal.

"Poultry" can be chicken, turkey, duck or other birds, so every poultry wording could include chicken. Generic animal terms don't name a species at all. Joint ingredients like chondroitin and hyaluronic acid can be made from chicken tissue or from other sources.

Fats, oils and hydrolyzed forms: less protein, not none

Food allergies are reactions to proteins (Merck Veterinary Manual), and processing changes how much intact protein an ingredient carries. Pawfood rates these as reduced carryover: chicken fat, hydrolyzed chicken protein, hydrolyzed chicken liver, natural chicken flavor. Reduced is not zero. Fats and oils can hold traces of protein, and hydrolysis breaks proteins into smaller pieces without removing every reactive fragment. Whether your pet can have these forms is a question for your vet.

How often it's reported in dogs and cats

Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud (2016) pooled published studies of dogs and cats with food allergy. The reported allergen sources included chicken in about 15% of dogs and about 5% of cats. Many animals react to more than one food, so the figures overlap. They describe those studies, not your own pet's odds.

Label checklist for chicken

  • Read the full ingredients list, not the front of the pack. Under AAFCO naming rules a "beef dinner" needs only 25% beef, and "with" or "flavor" names need far less.
  • Check every name in the table above, including fats, broths, digests and plasma.
  • Treat unnamed wordings ("meat", "animal fat", "natural flavor") as unknown and ask the manufacturer what they are made from.
  • Read inside parentheses and the vitamin and mineral part of the list.
  • Look for chicken fat and chicken broth in recipes led by fish, lamb or beef; they are easy to miss.
  • Recheck each new bag and each product separately: recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.

Treats, toppers and chews

Jerky, freeze-dried bites, dental chews, pill pockets and toppers often use chicken or chicken flavor. Check treats, chews, toppers and flavored supplements like food, and ask your vet before giving any during an elimination diet.

How Pawfood flags chicken

When chicken is on a pet's avoid list, Pawfood compares each ingredient it recognizes with the lists above:

  • A listed chicken ingredient: Match found if you saved chicken as a vet-confirmed allergy or as something you just avoid; Worth a closer look if you saved it as a suspected sensitivity.
  • A reduced-carryover form (fat, oil, hydrolyzed, flavor): still a match for a vet-confirmed allergy, with a note that it carries less protein.
  • A term that might mean chicken: Worth a closer look, because the label doesn't name the source.
  • No recorded match: nothing on the listed ingredients matched your records. It doesn't mean the food is allergen-free; labels can be incomplete and recipes change.

Words Pawfood doesn't recognize are shown as unrecognized, so check those against the label photo yourself.

Talk to your vet

Pawfood explains labels; it doesn't diagnose, treat or prescribe diets. Food allergy is usually confirmed with an elimination diet and food challenge run by a vet, so talk to yours before removing chicken or switching foods. If your pet has trouble breathing, facial swelling, repeated vomiting or collapses, contact a vet or emergency clinic right away.
