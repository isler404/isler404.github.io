Poultry allergy in dogs and cats: chicken, turkey, duck and other birds on the label

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

If your vet has confirmed a poultry allergy in your dog or cat, or you simply avoid poultry, the ingredients list is where you check. This page lists the label names that always mean poultry, the vaguer terms that might, and how Pawfood flags both.

What counts as poultry

Poultry in Pawfood means any farmed bird: chicken, turkey, duck, goose, quail, pheasant and guinea fowl, plus ingredients labeled only as "poultry". Some owners avoid the whole group on their vet's advice; ask yours whether that applies. Ostrich and egg are separate families.

Terms that might mean poultry

These wordings can contain poultry, but the label doesn't say exactly what they come from: chondroitin sulfate, natural flavor, gravy, meat, meat and animal derivatives, meat by-product meal, animal protein, hydrolyzed animal protein, animal fat, animal digest, liver, animal plasma, blood meal, hemoglobin, bone broth, broth, bone meal.

None of these wordings names a species. Meat, animal fat, digest, liver and broth could come from birds or mammals. A label that says only "poultry" tells you it's a bird, not which one.

Fats, oils and hydrolyzed forms: less protein, not none

Food allergies are reactions to proteins (Merck Veterinary Manual), and processing changes how much intact protein an ingredient carries. Pawfood rates these as reduced carryover: chicken fat, hydrolyzed chicken protein, hydrolyzed chicken liver, natural chicken flavor, turkey fat, hydrolyzed turkey protein, natural turkey flavor, duck fat, hydrolyzed duck protein, natural duck flavor, goose fat, poultry fat, hydrolyzed poultry protein, hydrolyzed poultry liver, natural poultry flavor. Reduced is not zero. Fats and oils can hold traces of protein, and hydrolysis breaks proteins into smaller pieces without removing every reactive fragment. Whether your pet can have these forms is a question for your vet.

How often it's reported in dogs and cats

Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud (2016) pooled published studies of dogs and cats with food allergy. The reported allergen sources included chicken in about 15% of dogs and about 5% of cats. Many animals react to more than one food, so the figures overlap. They describe those studies, not your own pet's odds. Turkey, duck and the other birds are not on the main lists; the chicken figures don't automatically apply to them.

Label checklist for poultry

  • Read the full ingredients list, not the front of the pack. Under AAFCO naming rules a "beef dinner" needs only 25% beef, and "with" or "flavor" names need far less.
  • Check every name in the table above, including fats, broths, digests and plasma.
  • Treat unnamed wordings ("meat", "animal fat", "natural flavor") as unknown and ask the manufacturer what they are made from.
  • Read inside parentheses and the vitamin and mineral part of the list.
  • Count each bird by name: duck fat in a "salmon" recipe is still poultry.
  • Recheck each new bag and each product separately: recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.

Treats, toppers and chews

Duck and chicken jerky, turkey necks, chicken feet, poultry broths and freeze-dried toppers are common in treat aisles. Check treats, chews, toppers and flavored supplements like food, and ask your vet before giving any during an elimination diet.

How Pawfood flags poultry

When poultry is on a pet's avoid list, Pawfood compares each ingredient it recognizes with the lists above:

  • A listed poultry ingredient: Match found if you saved poultry as a vet-confirmed allergy or as something you just avoid; Worth a closer look if you saved it as a suspected sensitivity.
  • A reduced-carryover form (fat, oil, hydrolyzed, flavor): still a match for a vet-confirmed allergy, with a note that it carries less protein.
  • A term that might mean poultry: Worth a closer look, because the label doesn't name the source.
  • No recorded match: nothing on the listed ingredients matched your records. It doesn't mean the food is allergen-free; labels can be incomplete and recipes change.

Words Pawfood doesn't recognize are shown as unrecognized, so check those against the label photo yourself.

Talk to your vet

Pawfood explains labels; it doesn't diagnose, treat or prescribe diets. Food allergy is usually confirmed with an elimination diet and food challenge run by a vet, so talk to yours before removing poultry or switching foods. If your pet has trouble breathing, facial swelling, repeated vomiting or collapses, contact a vet or emergency clinic right away.
