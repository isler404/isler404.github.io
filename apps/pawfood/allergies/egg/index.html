Egg allergy in dogs and cats: what to check on the label

Last updated 7 November 2026 · By Pawfood

If your vet has confirmed a egg allergy in your dog or cat, or you simply avoid egg, the ingredients list is where you check. This page lists the label names that always mean egg, the vaguer terms that might, and how Pawfood flags both.

What counts as egg

Egg covers whole egg, dried egg product, yolk, egg white, hydrolyzed egg protein, eggshell membrane, and quail and duck eggs. Pawfood keeps egg separate from chicken: an egg allergy and a chicken allergy are different, and a pet can have one without the other. Your vet will say whether to avoid both.

Terms that might mean egg

These wordings can contain egg, but the label doesn't say exactly what they come from: lecithin, natural flavor, eggshell, animal protein, pasta, bread.

Lecithin can be made from egg yolk, soy or sunflower. Eggshell is mostly mineral, but traces of membrane protein may remain. Animal protein and natural flavor don't name their source, and pasta or bread may contain egg.

Fats, oils and hydrolyzed forms: less protein, not none

Food allergies are reactions to proteins (Merck Veterinary Manual), and processing changes how much intact protein an ingredient carries. Pawfood rates these as reduced carryover: hydrolyzed egg protein, eggshell membrane. Reduced is not zero. Fats and oils can hold traces of protein, and hydrolysis breaks proteins into smaller pieces without removing every reactive fragment. Whether your pet can have these forms is a question for your vet.

How often it's reported in dogs and cats

Mueller, Olivry and Prélaud (2016) pooled published studies of dogs and cats with food allergy. The reported allergen sources included egg in about 4% of dogs. Many animals react to more than one food, so the figures overlap. They describe those studies, not your own pet's odds.

Label checklist for egg

  • Read the full ingredients list, not the front of the pack. Under AAFCO naming rules a "beef dinner" needs only 25% beef, and "with" or "flavor" names need far less.
  • Check every name in the table above, including fats, broths, digests and plasma.
  • Treat unnamed wordings ("meat", "animal fat", "natural flavor") as unknown and ask the manufacturer what they are made from.
  • Read inside parentheses and the vitamin and mineral part of the list.
  • Dried egg product is common in dry foods and is easy to overlook mid-list.
  • Ask the manufacturer where "lecithin" comes from when the source isn't stated.
  • Recheck each new bag and each product separately: recipes change, and shared equipment isn't listed.

Treats, toppers and chews

Egg-glazed biscuits, freeze-dried egg toppers and eggshell membrane joint chews are common sources. Check treats, chews, toppers and flavored supplements like food, and ask your vet before giving any during an elimination diet.

How Pawfood flags egg

When egg is on a pet's avoid list, Pawfood compares each ingredient it recognizes with the lists above:

  • A listed egg ingredient: Match found if you saved egg as a vet-confirmed allergy or as something you just avoid; Worth a closer look if you saved it as a suspected sensitivity.
  • A reduced-carryover form (fat, oil, hydrolyzed, flavor): still a match for a vet-confirmed allergy, with a note that it carries less protein.
  • A term that might mean egg: Worth a closer look, because the label doesn't name the source.
  • No recorded match: nothing on the listed ingredients matched your records. It doesn't mean the food is allergen-free; labels can be incomplete and recipes change.

Words Pawfood doesn't recognize are shown as unrecognized, so check those against the label photo yourself.

Talk to your vet

Pawfood explains labels; it doesn't diagnose, treat or prescribe diets. Food allergy is usually confirmed with an elimination diet and food challenge run by a vet, so talk to yours before removing egg or switching foods. If your pet has trouble breathing, facial swelling, repeated vomiting or collapses, contact a vet or emergency clinic right away.
