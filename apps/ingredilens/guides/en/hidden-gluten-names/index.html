Hidden gluten on food labels, names to look for

Updated 29 October 2026

Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley and rye and in grains bred from them. For people with coeliac disease, even small amounts damage the gut, so the gluten-free diet has to be strict and lifelong. Wheat is easy to spot. Barley and rye often hide behind other names, especially on US labels.

How gluten appears on labels

  • EU and UK: "cereals containing gluten" are one of the 14 allergens. The grain must be named and emphasised: "wheat flour", "barley malt extract", "rye". A label does not have to say "gluten" itself.
  • US: only wheat is a major allergen. Barley, rye and oats are listed by name in the ingredients but are not flagged in the "Contains" line. A US product with "Contains: milk" can still contain barley malt.

Names that mean gluten

Wheat and its relatives:

  • wheat, wheat flour, wholemeal, wheat starch (unless labelled gluten-free), wheat germ, wheat bran
  • spelt, khorasan (Kamut), einkorn, emmer, durum, farro, freekeh
  • semolina, farina, couscous, bulgur, graham flour, atta, seitan (wheat gluten)
  • breadcrumbs, panko, croutons, rusk (in sausages), batter
  • triticale (wheat-rye hybrid)

Barley and rye:

  • barley, pearl barley, malt, malt extract, malt flavouring, malt syrup, malted milk
  • brewer's yeast and most beer, lager, ale and stout (unless labelled gluten-free)
  • rye, rye flour, pumpernickel

Often contains gluten (check the full list):

  • soy sauce (usually brewed with wheat; tamari is often but not always wheat-free)
  • gravy granules, stock cubes, soups and sauces thickened with flour
  • seasoning mixes, crisps with malt vinegar flavour, coatings on chips and nuts
  • liquorice, some sweets and chocolate bars with wafer or biscuit
  • communion wafers, play dough (for children: allergies at school)

Ingredients that sound risky but are usually fine

These come from wheat or barley but are processed so heavily that the gluten is removed. EU law exempts some of them from allergen labelling, and coeliac organisations generally consider them suitable:

  • glucose syrup, dextrose and maltodextrin, even from wheat
  • distilled spirits such as whisky and vodka (distillation removes the protein)
  • wheat starch in products labelled gluten-free (specially processed)
  • barley malt vinegar in the small amounts used in food: Coeliac UK considers it acceptable, but check if you are unsure

Ingredients such as caramel colour (E150), modified starch and dextrin are only a problem if the label names wheat, barley or rye as the source. In the EU and UK this is required: "modified starch (wheat)".

"Gluten-free" is voluntary. Many naturally gluten-free foods (rice, potatoes, plain meat) do not say it. Some products carry a certification mark, such as the Crossed Grain symbol licensed by Coeliac UK and other European coeliac societies, which involves independent audits.

"No gluten-containing ingredients" is a different claim used by some restaurants in the UK. It is not the same as "gluten-free" because cross-contamination may not be controlled. See eating out with coeliac disease.

"May contain gluten"

A "may contain wheat" or "made in a factory that handles gluten" line is voluntary and has no legal threshold. See what "may contain" means. Coeliac UK advises people with coeliac disease to avoid products with a "may contain gluten" warning. Products labelled gluten-free have been tested against the 20 ppm limit.

Coeliac disease, wheat allergy and gluten sensitivity

  • Coeliac disease is an autoimmune condition. It is diagnosed with blood tests and usually a biopsy, while you are still eating gluten, so do not cut out gluten before testing.
  • Wheat allergy is an immune reaction to wheat proteins. It can be immediate and severe, and people with it may tolerate barley and rye.
  • Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity is a diagnosis made only after the other two have been ruled out.

Talk to your doctor before changing your diet. The NHS page on coeliac disease explains the tests.

Frequently asked questions

What does "gluten-free" mean on a label?

In the EU, the UK and the US, "gluten-free" means the food contains less than 20 mg of gluten per kg (20 parts per million). In the EU and UK, "very low gluten" means no more than 100 mg/kg.

Is malt gluten-free?

No. Malt is usually made from barley, which contains gluten. Malt extract, malt flavouring and malted milk are not suitable on a gluten-free diet unless the product is labelled gluten-free.

Is glucose syrup made from wheat safe for coeliac disease?

Wheat-derived glucose syrup, dextrose and maltodextrin are so highly processed that they are exempt from EU allergen labelling. Coeliac UK considers them suitable for people with coeliac disease.

Are oats gluten-free?

Oats do not contain gluten in the same way wheat does, but standard oats are often contaminated with wheat or barley. Only oats sold as gluten-free are produced to avoid this. A small number of people with coeliac disease also react to pure oats, so ask your doctor or dietitian.
