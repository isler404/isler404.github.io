Yuka alternative for food allergies: what to use

Updated 29 October 2026 · By Enescan İşler

In short: Yuka rates the overall health impact of food and cosmetics, which is useful for healthier shopping. People with food allergies or coeliac disease also need a check of their own allergens, "may contain" statements and products missing from databases, which is what a label-reading allergen app like IngrediLens is for. Many people use both, and still read the pack.

Yuka is one of the best-known product scanner apps, and many people with food allergies try it first. It does its own job well. But "is this product healthy?" and "does this product contain something I react to?" are different questions. This page explains the difference fairly, so you can decide what to use, or whether to use both.

What Yuka does well

According to Yuka's own App Store listing and website (checked in October 2026):

  • You scan the barcode of a food or personal care product and see a rating of its impact on health, with details explaining the analysis.
  • Food products are scored on three criteria: nutritional quality, additives and whether the product is organic.
  • Cosmetics are scored by analysing all their ingredients, with each ingredient given a risk level.
  • When a product scores poorly, Yuka suggests similar, better-rated products.
  • Yuka says it is independent: brands cannot influence scores, and there is no in-app advertising.
  • Its listing states a database of about 4 million food and 2 million cosmetic products.
  • Its website lists Premium features including offline mode, product search and dietary preferences that detect palm oil, gluten or lactose.

For general healthier shopping, that is a strong package, and a quick score is genuinely useful.

What people with allergies or coeliac disease need

An allergy check is not a score. It is a yes, no or "maybe" about specific substances, for specific people. In practice that means:

  1. Your own allergen list. Not just gluten or lactose, but whatever you avoid: peanuts, tree nuts, sesame, mustard, celery, lupin, molluscs, sulphites, or a custom item such as kiwi. See the 14 EU allergens.
  2. Other names. Casein and whey for milk, albumen for egg, spelt or semolina for wheat. See hidden milk names and hidden gluten names.
  3. "May contain" detection. Precautionary statements such as "may contain traces of nuts" sit outside the ingredient list. Many people with severe allergies avoid these products, following their allergist's advice. See what "may contain" means.
  4. Several people. A family may have a child with a nut allergy and an adult with coeliac disease. Separate profiles avoid mistakes.
  5. Products missing from databases. Local bakery items, imported foods and new recipes may have no barcode entry, or an old one. Reading the label photo checks the pack you are holding.
  6. Cosmetics as INCI. Skincare uses Latin and chemical names (for example Arachis Hypogaea Oil for peanut oil). See reading cosmetic ingredient lists.

Should you use both?

Often, yes. A sensible routine is:

  • Use Yuka if you want to compare products on nutrition, additives or cosmetic ingredients in general.
  • Use an allergen app to check the ingredient list and precautionary statement against your own allergens and those of your family.
  • Read the pack yourself every time, especially after a recipe change.

A product can score "excellent" for nutrition and still contain milk, or carry a "may contain nuts" warning. Equally, a poor score does not mean a product contains your allergen.

Frequently asked questions

Is Yuka good for food allergies?

Yuka is built to rate the overall health impact of food and cosmetics. Its official pages describe Premium dietary preferences for palm oil, gluten and lactose. If you need to check other allergens, "may contain" warnings or several family members, add a dedicated allergen app and always read the pack.

Can I use Yuka and an allergen app together?

Yes. They answer different questions. Yuka tells you how a product scores on nutrition, additives and organic status; an allergen app tells you whether the ingredient list mentions something you need to avoid.

What if the barcode is not in any database?

A barcode app cannot show a product it does not know. A label-reading app reads the printed ingredient list with the camera, so it can check local, imported or new products that are missing from databases.

Does a good health score mean a product is free from my allergen?

No. A health or nutrition score says nothing about whether a product contains or may contain a specific allergen. Always check the ingredient list and the precautionary statement.
