Eating out with coeliac disease, a practical guide

Updated 29 October 2026

With coeliac disease, the only treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet. At home you control the kitchen. In a restaurant you depend on someone else understanding that a few crumbs matter. This guide covers how to choose a place, what to ask, and where gluten slips in.

Why "a little bit" matters

In coeliac disease, gluten triggers an immune reaction that damages the lining of the small intestine. This can happen even without obvious symptoms. That is why the legal "gluten-free" limit is so low: 20 mg of gluten per kg of food in the EU, UK and US. A careless crumb from a shared toaster can be enough to cause a problem. Wheat allergy is different and can cause immediate reactions. If you are not sure which you have, ask your doctor.

Choosing where to eat

  • Look for accreditation. Many coeliac societies audit restaurants: Coeliac UK's GF accreditation, AIC's Alimentazione Fuori Casa in Italy, programmes run by coeliac associations in Spain, Portugal, France, Germany and elsewhere. Their apps and websites list approved places.
  • Read the menu wording. In the UK, a dish described as "gluten-free" must meet the 20 mg/kg limit, so the kitchen has to control cross-contamination. "No gluten-containing ingredients" (NGCI) only means the recipe has no gluten. It is not suitable if cross-contamination matters to you, which it does for coeliac disease.
  • Book ahead and mention coeliac disease when you reserve. Kitchens can prepare better at quieter times.
  • Choose simple food. Grilled meat or fish with plain vegetables and rice is easier to control than battered, breaded or heavily sauced dishes.
  • Naturally gluten-free cuisines can help: many Mexican (corn tortillas), Indian (rice, dal, but watch for asafoetida mixed with wheat flour), Thai and Japanese dishes, as long as you check sauces.

Questions to ask the staff

  1. "I have coeliac disease. I can't have wheat, barley or rye, even in small amounts. Can the kitchen prepare a dish for me separately?"
  2. "Is the sauce / gravy / dressing thickened with flour or made with soy sauce or beer?"
  3. "Do you have a separate fryer for gluten-free food?"
  4. "Is the pasta / rice cooked in fresh water, not the water used for regular pasta?"
  5. "Is the grill or plancha shared with bread or breaded food?"
  6. "Are the chips / fries / potatoes dusted with flour or coated?"
  7. "Is the gluten-free bread toasted in a separate toaster or in a toaster bag?"

If answers are vague or the staff seem unsure, it is safer to choose something else or eat somewhere else.

Oats are a special case. Only oats labelled gluten-free are suitable, and some people with coeliac disease react to those too. See hidden gluten names.

Travelling and language

Abroad, carry a short gluten-free restaurant card in the local language. It should explain that you cannot eat wheat, barley, rye or contaminated oats, and that cross-contamination matters. See restaurant allergy cards. Useful words: sin gluten and, in Argentina, sin TACC (Spanish), sem glúten (Portuguese), glutenfrei (German), sans gluten (French), senza glutine (Italian). In Italy, pharmacies and many supermarkets stock gluten-free products, and Italian law treats coeliac disease as a recognised condition, so awareness is generally high.

If you get "glutened"

Accidental exposure happens even to careful people. Symptoms vary. Note what you ate and where, and tell your doctor or dietitian if symptoms are severe or keep coming back. A reaction diary helps you spot patterns. Do not take new medicines without advice.

Frequently asked questions

Is "gluten-free" on a menu the same as on a pack?

In the UK and EU, a business that describes food as gluten-free must meet the same 20 mg/kg limit as packaged food, which means controlling cross-contamination. Some restaurants use "no gluten-containing ingredients" instead, which does not promise that.

Are chips gluten-free?

Potatoes are, but chips are often fried in the same oil as battered fish, breaded foods or onion rings, which contaminates them with gluten. Ask whether there is a dedicated fryer.

Can I just pick the croutons off a salad?

No. The salad has already been in contact with gluten. Ask for a fresh dish made without them.

Are there gluten-free certification schemes for restaurants?

Yes. Many national coeliac societies run accreditation for restaurants, for example Coeliac UK's gluten-free accreditation, the Italian Coeliac Association's AFC network, and programmes in Spain and other countries.
