Hidden milk on labels, other names for dairy

Updated 29 October 2026

Milk is one of the most common food allergens, especially in babies and young children. It is also one of the easiest to miss on a label. Dairy turns up in breads, crisps, processed meats, medicines and "plant-based" looking products under names that do not sound like milk.

Milk allergy or lactose intolerance?

They are very different, and the label reading is different too.

Milk allergyLactose intolerance
What reactsThe immune system, to milk proteins (casein, whey)The gut, to milk sugar (lactose), because of too little lactase enzyme
Typical symptomsHives, swelling, vomiting, wheezing; can be severe (anaphylaxis)Bloating, wind, stomach pain, diarrhoea; uncomfortable but not dangerous
Small amountsCan matter, depending on the personMany people tolerate small amounts
Lactose-free productsNot suitable (still contain milk protein)Suitable
Butter, hard cheeseNot suitableOften tolerated (low in lactose)

If you are not sure which one you have, see your doctor. A reaction diary can help that conversation. For babies with suspected cows' milk allergy, the NHS advises speaking to a GP or health visitor before changing feeds.

Names that mean milk

Proteins and protein ingredients:

  • casein, caseinates (sodium caseinate, calcium caseinate), rennet casein
  • whey, whey protein, whey powder, whey protein concentrate or isolate
  • lactalbumin, lactoglobulin, lactoferrin
  • milk protein, milk solids, skimmed milk powder, dried milk

Dairy foods:

  • butter, butterfat, butter oil, ghee, buttermilk
  • cream, soured cream, crème fraîche, curd, quark, yogurt, kefir, skyr
  • cheese of all kinds, including paneer, ricotta and mascarpone
  • custard, milk chocolate, white chocolate, fudge, toffee, caramel (often made with butter or cream)
  • condensed and evaporated milk, dulce de leche, malted milk

Sugars and others:

  • lactose (for an allergy, lactose can carry traces of milk protein; in the EU and UK lactose is part of the milk allergen entry)
  • "natural flavouring" or "butter flavour" can sometimes contain milk. In the EU, UK and US, milk must still be declared.

Names that sound like milk but usually are not

  • lactic acid (E270), lactates (E325–E327): usually from fermented sugar
  • cocoa butter, coconut milk, shea butter, peanut butter
  • cream of tartar (from grapes)
  • calcium, unless it says "milk calcium"

In the EU and UK, an ingredient made from milk must be linked to milk on the label. In the US the source must also be named, for example "lactic acid starter culture (milk)".

Label traps

  • "Non-dairy" and "dairy-free": these are marketing words. Some US "non-dairy" creamers contain caseinate, which must be identified as a milk derivative. Read the ingredients.
  • Vegan claims: vegan products are made without animal ingredients, but they may be made on shared lines and carry "may contain milk". See what "may contain" means.
  • Goat, sheep and buffalo milk: these count as milk. Under FDA guidance from January 2025, US labels should name the animal, such as "goat milk".
  • Medicines and supplements: lactose is a common filler in tablets. Ask your pharmacist if milk protein traces matter for you.
  • Restaurants: butter is often brushed on grilled meat, bread and vegetables, and is used in sauces and mashed potato. Ask how a dish is finished.

Frequently asked questions

Is lactose-free milk OK for a milk allergy?

No. Lactose-free milk has the milk sugar broken down, but it still contains milk proteins (casein and whey), which cause allergic reactions. It is meant for lactose intolerance, not milk allergy.

Does "non-dairy" mean milk-free?

Not always. In the US, some products labelled "non-dairy", such as coffee creamers, contain caseinate, a milk protein. The allergen declaration on the label must still mention milk.

Are lactic acid, cocoa butter and cream of tartar dairy?

No. Lactic acid (E270) is normally made by fermenting sugars, cocoa butter is fat from cocoa beans, and cream of tartar is a grape by-product. In the EU and UK, milk would have to be emphasised if it were the source.

Can people with cow's milk allergy drink goat or sheep milk?

Usually not. The proteins are very similar and most people with cow's milk allergy react to them too. Allergy organisations advise against using them as a substitute unless an allergist says otherwise.
