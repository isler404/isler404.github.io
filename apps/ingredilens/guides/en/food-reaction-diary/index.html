How to keep a food reaction diary to find triggers

Updated 29 October 2026

You had hives after lunch, or your skin flared after a new moisturiser, but you are not sure what caused it. A reaction diary turns vague memories into something your doctor can work with. It is simple to keep and is one of the most useful things you can bring to an allergy appointment.

Important: if someone has signs of a severe reaction (difficulty breathing, swelling of the throat or tongue, feeling faint), use their adrenaline auto-injector if they have one and call emergency services. The diary can wait.

Why a diary helps

Doctors diagnose food allergy mainly from your history: what happened, how quickly and after what. Tests such as skin prick tests or specific IgE blood tests are then used to confirm or rule out suspects. A good diary helps your doctor:

  • spot patterns (the same ingredient in several products, reactions only after exercise)
  • tell the difference between immediate allergic reactions, which usually come within minutes to about two hours, and delayed symptoms that may point to an intolerance or another cause
  • choose which tests make sense and avoid unnecessary ones

What to write down

For every reaction, record:

WhatExample
Date and time of eating or using14 Oct, 12:30
Exactly what it was"Chicken satay wrap, café X" or brand and product name
IngredientsPhoto of the label, or ask the restaurant
How muchTwo bites / whole portion
When symptoms started15 minutes later
SymptomsItchy mouth, hives on neck, stomach cramps
Severity and durationModerate, about 2 hours
What you didAntihistamine at 13:00
Other factorsRan to the station after lunch; had a cold; took ibuprofen in the morning

Cofactors such as exercise, alcohol, some painkillers (NSAIDs), illness, stress and lack of sleep can make a reaction more likely or more severe. That is why they are worth noting.

For skin reactions to cosmetics, note the product, where you applied it, how long after the rash started, and keep the packaging. See reading cosmetic ingredient lists.

Also note what you tolerated

It is just as useful to note foods you ate without problems, especially if they contain an ingredient you suspected. If you ate wheat bread every day without symptoms, wheat is less likely to be the cause of a reaction to a sandwich. The filling becomes the main suspect.

Common mistakes

  • Cutting out lots of foods on your own. Restrictive diets can cause nutritional problems, especially in children, and can make diagnosis harder. Coeliac disease tests, for example, need you to keep eating gluten until testing is done.
  • Reintroducing a food that caused a severe reaction "to test it". Food challenges should only be done under medical supervision.
  • Trusting home test kits. The NHS and allergy organisations warn that tests such as IgG food intolerance tests, hair analysis, kinesiology and "vega" testing are not reliable for diagnosing allergies.
  • Recording only the main ingredient. "Pasta" is not enough. The sauce may contain milk, egg, nuts or mustard. Our guides list hidden milk, egg and nut names.
  • Forgetting "may contain". A reaction to a product that only had a precautionary warning is important information for your allergist. See what "may contain" means.

Taking the diary to your appointment

Bring the diary, photos of labels, and any products or packaging that caused a reaction. Write down your questions beforehand, for example:

  • Is this likely to be an allergy, an intolerance or something else?
  • Which tests do I need?
  • Do I need an adrenaline auto-injector and an action plan?
  • Should I avoid "may contain" products?

Frequently asked questions

What should I write in a food reaction diary?

What you ate or used (with brand and ideally a photo of the ingredient list), how much, when, what symptoms appeared and how soon, how long they lasted, how severe they were, any medicine you took, and other factors such as exercise, alcohol, illness or painkillers.

How long should I keep a food diary?

Long enough to capture several reactions, often a few weeks, but follow your doctor's advice. Bring it to your appointment.

Can a food diary diagnose a food allergy?

No. A diary helps your doctor decide which tests to do, such as skin prick tests, blood tests for specific IgE, or a supervised food challenge. Diagnosis needs a medical assessment.

Are home food intolerance tests reliable?

Allergy specialists and the NHS advise against commercial tests such as IgG food intolerance tests, hair analysis or kinesiology, because they are not validated for diagnosing food allergy and can lead to unnecessary restricted diets.
