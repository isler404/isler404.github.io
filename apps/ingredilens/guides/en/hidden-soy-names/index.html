Hidden soy on food labels, other names for soya

Updated 29 October 2026 · By Enescan İşler

In short: Soy can appear as soya, soy lecithin (E322), edamame, miso, tempeh, tofu, textured vegetable protein (TVP) or hydrolysed soy protein, and on cosmetics as Glycine Soja. It hides in bread, chocolate, processed meats and meat substitutes. EU law exempts a few highly refined soy ingredients, such as fully refined soybean oil, from allergen labelling.

Soya is everywhere in processed food. It is used as a protein, an emulsifier, an oil, a flavour base and a meat substitute. In the EU and UK it is one of the 14 allergens, and in the US it is one of the 9 major allergens. It is often hidden behind technical names or in foods that do not look like soy at all.

How soy is labelled

  • EU and UK: soy must be emphasised in the ingredients list: "emulsifier (soya lecithin)", "hydrolysed soya protein".
  • US: soy must be named in the ingredient list ("lecithin (soy)") or in the "Contains" statement.

The words "soy" and "soya" mean the same thing. UK labels usually say soya, US labels say soy.

Names that mean soy

Whole and fermented soy foods:

  • soybean, soya bean, edamame, black soybeans, kinako (roasted soy flour)
  • tofu, tempeh, natto, yuba (tofu skin), okara (soy pulp)
  • miso, shoyu, tamari, soy sauce, teriyaki sauce, doenjang, gochujang (often contains soy)
  • soy milk, soya drink, soy yogurt, soy cheese

Ingredients made from soy:

  • soy lecithin (lecithins, E322, when from soy)
  • soy protein, soy protein isolate or concentrate, soy flour, soy grits
  • textured vegetable protein (TVP), textured soy protein, soy mince
  • hydrolysed soy protein, hydrolysed vegetable protein (check the source)
  • soybean oil (unrefined) and soy "butter"
  • mixed tocopherols (E306) and plant sterols, which can be soy-derived (exempt in the EU when refined)

Foods where soy often hides

  • Bread, biscuits and cakes (soy flour and lecithin improve texture)
  • Chocolate and sweets (soy lecithin as an emulsifier)
  • Processed meats: sausages, burgers, ham and nuggets often use soy protein as a binder
  • Meat substitutes and many vegan products
  • Sauces and stock cubes, especially Asian-style sauces, gravies and marinades
  • Protein bars and shakes, infant formula (soy-based), cereal bars
  • Tinned tuna in vegetable broth, some soups and ready meals
  • Restaurant cooking: deep-fat fryers may use soybean oil, and many Asian dishes contain soy sauce or miso

Exemptions: refined soy ingredients

EU law exempts a few soy derivatives because processing removes almost all of the protein. They do not have to be emphasised:

  • fully refined soybean oil and fat
  • natural mixed tocopherols (E306), natural D-alpha tocopherol and related forms from soy
  • vegetable oil-derived phytosterols and plant stanol esters from soy

In the US, highly refined oils are exempt from FALCPA allergen labelling. If you react to very small amounts, ask your allergist whether these matter for you.

Soy and other legumes

Soy is a legume, like peanuts, peas, lentils, chickpeas and lupin. Being allergic to one legume does not automatically mean you are allergic to the others, but some people do react to more than one. Pea protein and lupin flour are increasingly used in "free-from" and vegan products. Ask your allergist which legumes you need to avoid. Lupin is covered in the overlooked allergens, and peanuts in hidden nut names.

Soy in cosmetics

Soy appears on INCI ingredient lists as Glycine Soja (soybean oil, protein or extract) and Hydrolyzed Soy Protein. It is common in hair care and moisturisers. See reading cosmetic INCI lists.

Frequently asked questions

What are other names for soy on labels?

Soya, soybean, edamame, tofu, tempeh, miso, natto, shoyu, tamari, yuba, okara, soy lecithin, soy protein isolate, textured vegetable protein (TVP) and hydrolysed soy protein are common ones.

Is soy lecithin a problem for soy allergy?

Soy lecithin (E322) contains very little soy protein, and some people with soy allergy tolerate it. It is still labelled as a soy allergen in the EU, UK and US. Whether you need to avoid it is a question for your allergist.

Is soybean oil labelled as an allergen?

Fully refined soybean oil is exempt from allergen labelling in the EU and UK, and highly refined oils are exempt in the US, because refining removes almost all of the protein. Cold-pressed or unrefined soy oil is not exempt.

Is "vegetable protein" soy?

It can be. Hydrolysed vegetable protein and textured vegetable protein are often made from soy, sometimes from wheat or pea. In the EU, UK and US, soy must be named if it is the source.
