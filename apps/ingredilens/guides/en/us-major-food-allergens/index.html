The 9 major food allergens in the US (FALCPA and sesame)

Updated 29 October 2026 · By Enescan İşler

In short: US food labels must declare nine major allergens: milk, egg, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame, which was added from 1 January 2023 by the FASTER Act. Each must be named in the ingredient list or in a “Contains” statement. Barley, rye, mustard, celery and molluscs are not major allergens in the US.

In the United States, food labels must declare nine major food allergens. The rules come from the Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act of 2004 (FALCPA) and the Food Allergy Safety, Treatment, Education, and Research Act of 2021 (FASTER Act), which added sesame. The FDA enforces them for most packaged food. Meat, poultry and most egg products fall under the USDA.

Together these account for most serious food-allergic reactions in the US, according to the FDA. Other foods can still cause allergies.

How allergens appear on a US label

Each major allergen in a product must be named at least once, in one of two ways:

  • In the ingredient list, either as the common name ("milk", "wheat flour") or in parentheses after a technical name: "casein (milk)", "lecithin (soy)", "semolina (wheat)".
  • In a "Contains" statement directly after or next to the ingredient list, such as "Contains: Milk, Egg, Wheat." If a company uses a "Contains" statement, it must list all major allergens in the food.

The allergen must be declared even when it is in a flavoring, coloring or incidental additive. Unlike in the EU, there is no requirement for bold text. Some brands use bold anyway.

Tree nuts: which ones count

Under the January 2025 FDA guidance, the tree nuts that must be declared are a shorter list of commonly allergenic nuts, including almond, Brazil nut, cashew, hazelnut (filbert), macadamia, pecan, pine nut, pistachio and walnut. Coconut and several rarely eaten nuts were removed. If you are allergic to a nut that is not on the list, look for it as an ordinary ingredient name. Hidden names for nuts are in hidden peanut and tree nut names.

What US labels do not cover

  • Other allergens. Mustard, celery, lupin and molluscs (clams, mussels, squid) are not major allergens in the US, although they are in the EU. See the 14 EU allergens.
  • Barley, rye and oats. Only wheat is a major allergen. People with coeliac disease need to read for barley, rye and malt separately, or choose products labeled "gluten-free". See hidden gluten.
  • Highly refined oils. Oils made from an allergen (such as highly refined soybean or peanut oil) are exempt from allergen labeling.
  • Cross-contact. "May contain" is voluntary. See what "may contain" means.
  • Restaurant food. FALCPA covers packaged food. Restaurants are not required to label allergens, so ask staff directly.

Sesame since 2023

Sesame labeling became mandatory on 1 January 2023. Foods already on the market before that date did not have to be relabeled, so older stock could lack a sesame declaration for a while. The FDA has also noted that some manufacturers responded by adding sesame to products and labeling it, rather than separating production lines. A bread you once ate safely may now contain sesame, so re-read labels. More in sesame, mustard, celery and lupin.

Frequently asked questions

What are the 9 major food allergens in the US?

Milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame. Sesame became the ninth on 1 January 2023 under the FASTER Act.

Is coconut a tree nut on US labels?

Not any more. In its January 2025 allergen labeling guidance (Edition 5), the FDA narrowed the list of tree nuts that count as major allergens, and coconut is no longer on it. Someone allergic to coconut still needs to read the ingredient list.

Does goat milk count as milk for US allergen labels?

Yes. Under the FDA's January 2025 guidance, milk from goats, sheep and other ruminants counts as milk, and eggs from ducks, geese, quail and other fowl count as eggs. The FDA recommends naming the animal, for example "goat milk".

Do US labels have to warn about "may contain"?

No. Advisory statements such as "may contain" or "processed in a facility that also processes" are voluntary. The FDA says they should be truthful and not misleading, and should not replace good manufacturing practices.
